Friday, 9:20 in the morning. The fridge door will not shut on the first try, because a foil packet of dark meat is wedged against the shelf and the carcass is still on the counter where it landed at nine last night. The menu took three weeks and a group chat. Nobody has a plan for today.

The three days after the meal are the ones with real numbers attached: two hours on the counter, four days in the fridge, one temperature at the reheat. The tradition has a word for the same stretch.

What follows is a reading of that window, with the arithmetic holiday writing usually skips. It is not medical advice. Diarrhea past three days, a fever over 102°F, or vomiting that keeps you from holding water belongs to a doctor, not a kitchen.

## Two hours decide the rest of the week

The federal rule is blunt. Perishable food should not sit at room temperature more than two hours, and the clock starts when the food comes off the heat, not when the plates are cleared. Above 90°F, the limit drops to one hour.

Between 40 and 140°F bacteria multiply fast enough to matter, and a covered roasting pan sits in the middle of it for as long as it holds heat.

So the first job Thursday night is not dishes. It is division: carve the meat off the bone, spread it in shallow containers no more than two inches deep, get it cold. A quart of sliced turkey in a 38°F fridge drops through the danger zone in a couple of hours. A whole bird in a stockpot, still warm at the center, can sit in it until morning.

One inherited rule deserves retiring. "Let it cool completely before it goes in the fridge" was standard home-economics guidance for decades, and it is backwards. The fridge is a cooling machine. A pot left out to cool is a pot being cultured.

Confirm the fridge with a five-dollar thermometer rather than the dial on the door. It should read 40°F or lower, and plenty of American fridges run warm at the back of a full shelf in November.

## Four days is the line, and gravy is shorter

Cooked turkey keeps three to four days in the refrigerator. Stuffing does too, and so does the casserole. Gravy is the exception at one to two days, because a liquid at the bottom of a cold shelf warms and cools unevenly and gets reheated by the ladle every time the door opens.

Reheating has one target: 165°F throughout, the temperature that kills the live cells of whatever grew while the food was warm. Gravy goes further and wants a rolling boil.

That number does not cover everything, which is why the sniff test is not a test. The organism most often traced to reheated meat and gravy is Clostridium perfringens. It grows between 40 and 140°F and can double in as little as seven to ten minutes near the top of that range. The toxin it makes forms in your gut after you swallow it, not in the container, and it has no smell. A dish that passes the nose test can still cost you Friday night.

Which makes the freezer the answer. Pack half the meat on Friday, label it, move it in Saturday morning. Frozen turkey holds two to three months before the texture goes; the safety does not.

## The bones are the part people throw away

Most of the bird is still there, and the bones are what becomes stock. Cover the carcass with cold water in your largest pot, add an onion, a carrot, celery if it exists, and a splash of vinegar. Hold it at a bare simmer, 180 to 190°F, a bubble breaking the surface now and then rather than a rolling boil.

Two to four hours is enough. A hard boil for eight pulls out no more; it emulsifies fat into the liquid and gives you cloudy stock with a greasy mouth.

Strain, chill, lift the fat cap off in the morning, freeze in two-cup portions. The same two-hour clock applies here. The stockpot goes into shallow containers within two hours of coming off the heat, and the carcass goes into the pot or the trash tonight, not on Saturday.

## The tradition has a word for eating less after a feast (损谷, sǔn gǔ)

It does not mean detox.

The Treatise on Cold Damage (《伤寒论》), Zhang Zhongjing's clinical manual from around 220 CE, has a short passage about a recovering patient who is fed generously. The pulse has settled, but by dusk the person is mildly restless and irritable, because the spleen and stomach qi are still weak and cannot break down the grain. The instruction that follows is the entire prescription: reduce the grain, and it resolves (损谷则愈).

Read that without the illness. It describes a system that has run at capacity for six hours and needs a smaller load, which is where most people are on Friday morning. Sun Simiao, writing in the seventh century, advised eating often and little rather than all at once and much, and staying, in his phrase, a little hungry inside fullness (饱中饥，饥中饱).

In practice that looks like congee at nine, a plate half the size of Thursday's at one, and nothing between them. Warm, plain, seasoned lightly enough to taste like food. The [Ginger & Millet Congee](/recipes/congee-settled-stomach/) is the version this site keeps returning to, and Friday morning is what it was written for.

It does not look like the juice cleanse, the twenty-hour fast, or the penitential salad. All three are the same error in different clothes: answering a surplus with a subtraction large enough to become its own event.

## The tryptophan story is wrong in a useful way

Every November the bird gets blamed for the couch. Three ounces of roasted turkey carries roughly 250 to 300 milligrams of tryptophan, close to what chicken carries and well under a cup of cooked soybeans. If the amino acid were the mechanism, a chicken dinner would end the same way.

The sleepiness is volume, alcohol, and duration. A Thanksgiving plate runs well past a thousand calories, the wine arrives before the food, and the meal occupies four hours instead of forty.

There is a real tryptophan detail underneath, though. Tryptophan competes with other large neutral amino acids for the same transporter into the brain, so a plate of pure protein delivers it alongside its rivals and very little gets through. Carbohydrate raises insulin, which clears those rivals out of the blood, which is why the roll and the pie make the turkey more sedating rather than less. The stuffing is not a side dish. It is the delivery system.

None of which gets you out of the Friday you are in. Smaller warm meals, water, a walk, and a freezer doing what the fridge cannot.

Start with the two hours on Thursday night, because everything after depends on it and it costs fifteen minutes. If the leftovers are already past their fourth day, throw them out without negotiating. The bird cost twenty dollars. The week does not.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| The turkey sat out on the table for most of the evening | ☐ |
| I could not tell you the temperature of my refrigerator | ☐ |
| I reheat leftovers until they feel warm rather than to a number | ☐ |
| I feel heavy, foggy, or short-tempered for two or three days after a big meal | ☐ |
| My instinct after a feast is to skip meals or drink only juice | ☐ |
| The carcass goes in the trash rather than into a pot | ☐ |

Four or more and the three days after are worth planning alongside the menu. Two or fewer, and your kitchen is ahead of most. Rows one and three are the pair to watch: they are the only two on the list that can put you in bed rather than merely slow you down.

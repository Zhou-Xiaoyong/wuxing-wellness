The morning decides whether a plan survives. Dinner has company, a restaurant, somebody else's appetite. Breakfast is you at 7:10, alone, with four minutes and no interest in a project. That is why a plan that rearranges all three meals tends to be abandoned by Thursday, and why the one below asks for exactly one.

Most Chinese medicine meal plans arrive as a long pdf keyed to a body type you chose from an online quiz, and they want the entire day rebuilt around that label. The tradition underneath them was more modest. Food therapy (食疗, shí liáo) in the classical texts is usually a single preparation, a congee or a soup or a tea, eaten next to ordinary rice and vegetables. An addition, not a replacement menu.

Everything below is a traditional framework for cooking. It is not medical advice, not a therapeutic diet, and it diagnoses nothing.

## What Sun Simiao actually asked for

Sun Simiao, writing in 652, put the order of operations plainly in the food-therapy chapter of his *Emergency Formulas Worth a Thousand in Gold* (《备急千金要方·食治》). A physician should first find the source of the illness and what provoked it, then treat with food, and only when food fails prescribe medicine (以食治之，食疗不愈，然后命药).

Two things are worth pulling out of that sentence. Food comes first, but it comes after a diagnosis, not instead of one. And the unit of treatment is a dish. That is why the food-therapy chapters of the old books read like recipe collections and not like weekly menus.

For a modern kitchen, the second point is the useful one. One dish is a thing you can keep.

## One pot, fourteen mornings

The base is plain rice congee (白粥, bái zhōu), and the numbers matter more than the ingredients. Half a cup of jasmine or short-grain rice, rinsed twice until the water runs clear. Six cups of cold water. A teaspoon of neutral oil, which keeps the foam down. Bring it to a boil, knock it to the lowest heat that still moves the surface, and leave it 75 minutes, stirring at the twenty, forty and sixty marks so the base does not catch. That yields about four cups, enough for two mornings; reheat the second with a splash of water.

Li Shizhen's *Compendium of Materia Medica* (《本草纲目》) quotes a Song dynasty essay on congee that calls a bowl of it on an empty stomach the finest trick in eating (最为饮食之妙诀), mild and soft and agreeable to the stomach and intestines. The argument is not nutritional. It is about what a stomach can do before nine in the morning.

Then one addition a day, for two weeks.

| Morning | Add to the pot | What it is doing there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3 slices fresh ginger (生姜, shēng jiāng), last 10 minutes | For the chill you woke up with. Gingerol is the compound behind the burn. |
| 2 | 3 red dates (红枣, hóng zǎo), pitted and sliced | Sweetness the tradition files with the spleen. Also stops the pot tasting of nothing. |
| 3 | 1 piece dried tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí), soaked 10 minutes | The aromatic answer to a heavy dinner the night before. |
| 4 | 2 tbsp Job's tears (薏米, yì yǐ rén), soaked overnight | The damp-morning line. Chewy, so it needs the full soak. |
| 5 | 1 tbsp black sesame (黑芝麻, hēi zhī ma), ground | For the driest morning of the fortnight. Whole seeds pass through intact. |
| 6 | 5 dried longan (桂圆, guì yuán) | The morning after five hours of sleep. Sweet and warm, and easy to overdo. |
| 7 | Half a pear, diced, last 12 minutes | Scratchy throat, indoor heat, October air. Cooked pear, not raw. |
| 8 | 10 lotus seeds (莲子, lián zǐ), soaked, cores out | A settling morning. The green core is bitter; split the seed and flick it out. |
| 9 | 5 slices dried Chinese yam (山药, shān yào) | The plainest building addition on the list. Almost no taste of its own. |
| 10 | 3 tbsp cooked adzuki beans (红豆, hóng dòu) | The only legume line, and the only fiber in a bowl that otherwise has none. |
| 11 | 1 tbsp goji (枸杞, gǒu qǐ), off the heat | Color and a little sweetness. Boiling them wastes the point. |
| 12 | 2 walnuts (核桃, hé táo), broken up | For the first hard freeze. Fat and a bitter edge. |
| 13 | 2 scallion whites and 3 ginger slices, salted | A chill with no appetite. Savory rather than sweet. |
| 14 | Nothing. A pinch of salt and a teaspoon of sesame oil | The reset. Plain congee is the baseline you are measuring against. |

Four lines on that table are not for everyone. Skip Job's tears in pregnancy, which is the standard caution in the Chinese sources and not something to test. Skip longan when your throat is sore and hot, or when you have been sleeping badly and waking irritable, since it adds heat. Skip Chinese yam during a constipated stretch, because it is astringent. Skip the tangerine peel for a dry cough with nothing coming up; it dries you further.

## Three rules for the meals you did not plan

Lunch and dinner get filters instead of slots. Three of them cover most of the decisions.

**Cook it.** In the cold half of the year, cooked vegetables beat a cold pile of leaves, and both arguments point the same way. Heat breaks cell walls, so cooked carrots give up more beta-carotene and cooked tomatoes more lycopene. A crisp raw thing at midday in July is fine; nobody in the old books forbids salad.

**One pungent at the start, one sour at the table.** The *Plain Questions* (《素问·至真要大论》) draws the line this way: pungent and sweet disperse and belong to yang, sour and bitter drain and belong to yin (辛甘发散为阳，酸苦涌泄为阴). In practice, aromatics go into the oil before anything else, and vinegar or lemon goes on once the plate is off the heat. The same food reads as seasoned on less salt.

**Stop before the second helping.** The tradition's phrasing is to leave the stomach a third empty. There is no trial behind that number, and the mechanical version is duller and true: reflux and the three o'clock slump track with volume more than with which food it was.

## What is deliberately absent

No calorie target and no macro split. Nobody maintains a spreadsheet past the second week, and half a cup of rice is already the portion control.

No five-day reset and no liver flush. The liver detoxifies continuously and a week of juice does not improve on it.

No elimination of a food group unless a clinician has asked for one.

One limit is specific to this page. Rice porridge sits high on the glycemic index, around 78 in published tables, higher than cooked rice, because the starch has already been broken down. If you manage diabetes, halve the bowl, add the adzuki or Job's tears line, and treat the rest as a conversation with your clinician rather than a printable. With chronic kidney disease, the potassium in the beans and the yam is the same conversation.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I eat breakfast standing up, or not at all | ☐ |
| I have saved a meal plan and quit inside a week | ☐ |
| My dinners get decided at six in the evening | ☐ |
| I own a pot that can hold a low simmer for an hour | ☐ |
| I would rather change one meal than all three | ☐ |
| I have been eating the same breakfast for years | ☐ |

Four or more and the pot is the fix. If only the last one is true, you already own the habit; the fourteen lines give it somewhere to go.

## Tomorrow's pot

Buy a five-pound bag of rice, which is about thirteen cups dry and twenty-six mornings at half a cup. Cook the base tonight, cool it, and put it in the fridge; congee reheats better from cold than it cooks from scratch at dawn. Tomorrow, ginger.

One caveat. If what sent you looking for a meal plan is a symptom that has been there for weeks, a breakfast rotation is not the instrument for it. This is a way to make one meal of the day deliberate. It has no opinion about why your hands are cold.

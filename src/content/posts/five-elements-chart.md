Most people arrive at the five elements looking for the chart. Fair enough — the grid is the fastest way into the system, and once you've seen the columns line up, a great deal of Chinese medicine stops looking mysterious and starts looking like a filing system.

So here it is, with the correspondences that actually get used in practice rather than the exhaustive list. Then I'll explain how to read it, and — more importantly — how not to.

One note before the table. The five elements (五行, wǔ xíng) are not really "elements" in the Greek sense of basic substances. The second character means *movement* or *phase*, and "five phases" is the more accurate translation. These are five stages of a process: growth, peak, ripening, contraction, storage. The chart isn't a list of ingredients. It's a wheel.

And the standard caveat: this is a traditional framework for observing patterns and choosing habits, not a diagnostic tool. Nothing here is medical advice, and no chart replaces a clinician.

## The master five elements chart

| | Wood (木, mù) | Fire (火, huǒ) | Earth (土, tǔ) | Metal (金, jīn) | Water (水, shuǐ) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Season** | Spring | Summer | Late summer | Autumn | Winter |
| **Yin organ** | Liver | Heart | Spleen | Lungs | Kidneys |
| **Yang organ** | Gallbladder | Small intestine | Stomach | Large intestine | Bladder |
| **Emotion** | Anger (怒, nù) | Joy (喜, xǐ) | Overthinking (思, sī) | Grief (悲, bēi) | Fear (恐, kǒng) |
| **Flavor** | Sour (酸) | Bitter (苦) | Sweet (甘) | Pungent (辛) | Salty (咸) |
| **Color** | Green | Red | Yellow | White | Black |
| **Sense organ** | Eyes | Tongue | Mouth | Nose | Ears |
| **Tissue** | Tendons | Blood vessels | Muscles | Skin | Bones |
| **Climate** | Wind | Heat | Dampness | Dryness | Cold |
| **Direction** | East | South | Center | West | North |
| **Time of day** | 11pm–3am | 11am–3pm | 7am–11am | 3am–7am | 3pm–7pm |

A few of those rows deserve a footnote.

**The organs are systems, not anatomy.** The "spleen" of Chinese medicine covers digestion, nutrient extraction and muscle tone — it has almost nothing to do with the anatomical spleen a surgeon would remove. Same for "kidney," which in this framework includes reproduction, bone, hearing and constitutional vitality. Read every organ name as a functional category, and the chart makes far more sense.

**Earth's season is contested.** Some schemes give Earth late summer, that heavy humid stretch when growth stops and fruit ripens. Others place Earth in the eighteen-day transition at the end of every season, on the logic that all change passes through the center. Both are traditional; the second is arguably more elegant.

**The time column is the body clock.** Each organ system is said to have a two-hour peak. The famous one is 1 to 3 a.m. for the liver, which is why chronic 2 a.m. waking gets read as a Wood pattern.

## The two cycles that make the chart work

A static grid isn't the interesting part. The relationships are.

**The generating cycle** (生, shēng) — each element feeds the next:

> Wood feeds Fire → Fire creates Earth (ash) → Earth yields Metal (ore) → Metal carries Water (condensation) → Water grows Wood

This is the "mother and child" sequence. If a system is weak, the classical move is often to strengthen its mother rather than attack the symptom. Weak lungs? Feed Earth — fix the digestion. It's an early version of treating upstream.

**The controlling cycle** (克, kè) — each element restrains the one two steps ahead:

> Wood parts Earth (roots break soil) → Earth dams Water → Water quenches Fire → Fire melts Metal → Metal cuts Wood

This is the balancing mechanism, and it's what keeps the system from being a simple chain. Every element has something that feeds it, something it feeds, something that checks it, and something it checks. Four relationships each, and no element sits at the top.

When control goes wrong it goes wrong in two named ways: **overacting** (too much control — the classic example is stress and frustration, a Wood state, wrecking digestion, an Earth function) and **insulting** (control running backward, as when raging Fire scorches the Water that should cool it). If you've ever wondered why a stressful month reliably ruins your stomach, the chart had an answer for it two thousand years ago.

## How to read the chart without over-reading it

This is the part most beginner guides skip, and it's the part that keeps the framework useful.

**Don't treat the columns as a personality test.** A chart is a map of correspondences, not a diagnosis. Sour foods don't "fix" your liver, and eating yellow food on a Tuesday doesn't strengthen your spleen. The relationships are analogical — they organize observation, they don't run a mechanism.

**Expect to be a blend.** Nobody is one column. Most people have a dominant element, a strong second, and one that's quietly running low. Reading the chart as five personality boxes is the fastest way to get it wrong.

**Look for clusters, not single rows.** One dry morning doesn't mean depleted Metal. Dry skin *plus* a dry cough *plus* constipation *plus* a loss you haven't processed, all in autumn — that's a cluster, and clusters are what the framework is actually good at spotting.

**Use it forward, not backward.** The chart's best use is choosing habits: it's late autumn, the air is dry, so cook something moistening. That's a reasonable inference. Working backward from a symptom to a confident conclusion about an organ is not — that's a clinician's job, and a licensed one.

## Putting the chart to work in a week

The practical version is smaller than the grid suggests. Pick the current season's column and do three things from it.

- **Spring / Wood:** move your body daily, eat something sour and green, and deal with the thing you're irritated about instead of storing it.
- **Summer / Fire:** protect your sleep, add a little bitter (leafy greens, green tea), and don't let a good mood turn into three months of overcommitment.
- **Late summer / Earth:** eat warm cooked food on a regular schedule, walk after meals, and give worry a fixed container.
- **Autumn / Metal:** breathe deliberately, eat moistening foods against autumn dryness (秋燥), and clear one drawer.
- **Winter / Water:** sleep more, keep your lower back warm, eat dark mineral-rich food, and accept that resting is the assignment.

That's it. The chart has forty boxes; a good week uses three of them.

## Where the chart comes from

The five-phase system predates its medical use. It shows up in early Chinese cosmology as a way of organizing time, direction and political succession, and was systematically mapped onto the body in the Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon (《黄帝内经》), the foundational text compiled roughly two thousand years ago. Later works — most famously the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) of the sixteenth century — extended the correspondences to hundreds of foods and herbs.

Worth knowing: the correspondences were never perfectly fixed. Different classical schools assigned different foods, and the chart above reflects the mainstream consensus rather than a single canonical source. Anyone presenting the five elements as one settled, unanimous doctrine is oversimplifying.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| One season of the year reliably feels harder on my body than the others | ☐ |
| I recognize myself in one column more than the other four | ☐ |
| My digestion changes noticeably when I'm stressed | ☐ |
| I have a symptom cluster that always shows up together, not alone | ☐ |
| I'd get more from adjusting seasonal habits than from another supplement | ☐ |
| I want a framework for noticing patterns, not a diagnosis | ☐ |

Four or more checks and the five-element framework will earn its keep for you — mostly as a prompt to pay attention at the right time of year. Two or fewer and it's an interesting piece of cultural history, which is a perfectly good reason to read on.

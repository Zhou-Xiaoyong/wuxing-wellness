Lunch was at twelve. It is twenty past two and you can still feel it: a weight under the ribs, a belt that has moved a notch, a head that does not want to do arithmetic. Nothing hurts. You are carrying a meal that should have finished with you an hour ago.

Most writing about the Earth element answers the wrong question. Every page on the spleen tells you what to eat: warm, cooked, naturally sweet, millet and yam and squash. All of it is right. You can also eat those foods three times a day and feel like this at two, because what went wrong was not the ingredient. It was how much of it arrived at once.

The tradition thought about that amount early. The chapter on the twelve officials (《素问·灵兰秘典论》) names the spleen and stomach the granary officers, the pair from which the five flavors emerge (脾胃者，仓廪之官，五味出焉). A granary is not a warehouse. It is a facility defined by throughput, and by what happens to grain that arrives faster than the doors open.

The same book gives the failure mode in six characters: eat double, and the stomach and intestines are injured (饮食自倍，肠胃乃伤, 《素问·痹论》). Not eat badly. Double.

What follows is a traditional reading of that limit, and a week-long way to find your own number. It is not medical advice. Pain, bleeding, weight loss you did not try for, or a meal that sits for days is a clinician's question.

## The center moves food; it does not store it

One sentence in the chapter on the channels (《素问·经脉别论》) sets out the route: drink enters the stomach, its essence floats free and rises to the spleen, which scatters it upward to the lungs (饮入于胃，游溢精气，上输于脾，脾气散精，上归于肺).

Read it as plumbing rather than poetry and the useful part appears. Four handoffs, each with its own rate. Food that reaches the second and stops moving is not qi yet. The tradition calls it food stagnation (食积, shí jī) first, and if it sits long enough, dampness (湿, shī), the heavy foggy quality this site has covered elsewhere. The excess does not vanish. It waits, and it occupies the space the next meal needs.

An empty stomach holds about a quarter cup. After eating it is comfortable at roughly a quart, and at full distension two to four. The mechanism between those numbers is the part worth knowing: as food arrives, the upper stomach relaxes to make room. Fullness therefore is not generated by volume. It is generated by hormones and by feedback from the small intestine, and those arrive fifteen to thirty minutes late.

## Where the fourth cup goes

Most adults eat comfortably at around three cups of cooked food in a sitting, grain, vegetables and protein together. Treat that as an estimate to argue with by the end of the week. Three cups is a cupped handful of rice, two open hands of cooked vegetables, and a palm-sized piece of fish or chicken.

The ceiling is not level across a day. On the organ clock the stomach's window is seven to nine in the morning and the spleen's nine to eleven, the oldest surviving argument for eating the day's real meal early. Capacity follows the clock: widest at breakfast, narrowest after dark. Three cups that leave you light at eight in the morning will sit in you at eight at night.

Go to four and the surplus is not credited against tomorrow. Barbara Rolls' group at Penn State ran the cleanest version of this experiment repeatedly: serve a larger portion and people eat roughly thirty percent more at that meal, then eat just as much at the next one. The fourth cup is not stored as qi. It is the thing you feel at two in the afternoon. Sweet (甘) is Earth's flavor, and the classics mean grain-sweet, not sugar.

## A week to find your own ceiling

One test does most of the work, and it needs no calorie counting.

**The four-hour rule.** Note the hour you finish eating. When the next mealtime comes, are you still aware of the previous meal? Not hungry. Aware. That meal was over your ceiling, and the problem was the amount rather than the menu. Eat a quarter less of the same thing tomorrow and note that one too.

**Subtract a quarter, not a third.** Take your usual plate and remove about three-quarters of a cup. Eat slowly enough that twenty minutes pass before you decide anything. Satisfied is your new number. Genuinely hungry an hour later means add half of it back.

**Count cups, not calories.** Cooked volume is visible in a bowl. Calories are not, and the arithmetic wears out by Thursday.

**Put the recipe where the ceiling is lowest.** [Congee for a Settled Stomach](/recipes/congee-settled-stomach/) is the archetypal Earth meal for a structural reason: one cup of rice to six of water yields a large, warm, low-density bowl that fills without arriving faster than the center can move it.

## What lowers the ceiling

**Cold with the meal.** Iced water, a salad straight out of the refrigerator, a smoothie at seven. The tradition reads cold as a tax on the fire under the pot. The practical version is duller and just as real: cold slows gastric emptying, so the meal sits longer.

**Eight minutes at the counter.** A five-year cohort of roughly 60,000 Japanese adults found that self-described fast eaters carried higher body mass and larger waists than slow eaters, with a gradient across the middle group. Eating fast delivers the meal inside the window before the fullness signal has landed.

**Worry, in loops.** Earth's emotion is pensiveness (思, sī), the state of chewing a thought without swallowing it, and the tradition treats it as digestive work: the same system that transforms food is asked to transform a problem. [Overthinking and the Spleen](/blog/overthinking-spleen/) takes that loop apart.

**The meal after nine.** Capacity is lowest exactly when the American day schedules its largest meal, and two to three hours between dinner and lying down is standard advice for an unglamorous reason.

## What raises it

Warm, cooked and soft is the first move, and it is boring enough that people skip it looking for a supplement.

Chew past the point that feels necessary. No magic number exists, whatever the internet says; the point is mechanical breakdown before the handoff, and the home version is setting the fork down between bites.

Eat the vegetables and the protein first and the starch last. A small 2015 study found this order blunted the glucose response to an identical meal by a wide margin. One small study, so hold it loosely, but the sequence costs nothing.

Serve from the kitchen rather than the table. The portion on your plate is the one you will eat; the platter is the thing that refills it. Skip the smaller-plate trick while you are at it. Reviews of the plate-size experiments find the effect small and inconsistent, while the portion-size effect is one of the more dependable findings in the field.

Ten minutes of walking afterward, which the tradition frames as helping transformation and the modern reading calls a flatter glucose curve.

None of this asks you to give anything up. It asks you to arrive at the amount before the amount arrives at you.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I can still feel lunch at three in the afternoon | ☐ |
| I finish a plate in under ten minutes without deciding to | ☐ |
| I drink something cold with most meals | ☐ |
| Dinner is the largest meal of my day | ☐ |
| I eat past the point where I would have said "enough" | ☐ |
| A big meal leaves me heavy rather than satisfied | ☐ |

Four or more, and the amount is the lever rather than the menu. Run the four-hour rule for seven days before you change a single ingredient. If meals sit for a day instead of an afternoon, or if eating has turned into something you dread, stop reading your own signals and have someone read them properly.

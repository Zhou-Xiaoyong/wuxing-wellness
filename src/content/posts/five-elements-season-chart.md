The seasonal row is where five-element theory stops being a chart on a wall and starts being useful. Personality types are entertaining. Organ correspondences are interesting. But the season you're standing in is a fact, and it changes what your body is doing whether you pay attention or not.

Classical Chinese medicine treats the year as the primary rhythm a person lives inside. The Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon (《黄帝内经》) devotes early chapters to living in step with the seasons and warns, in effect, that fighting the year is how people get sick. Two thousand years later that reads less like mysticism and more like an early account of what we'd now call seasonal physiology.

Here's the chart, then the two things about it that surprise most Western readers — there are five seasons, not four, and the risky moments aren't the seasons themselves but the handoffs between them.

As always: a traditional framework for choosing habits, not medical advice or a diagnostic tool.

## The five elements season chart

| Season | Element | Organ system | Climate | Flavor to favor | Focus of the season |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Spring** | Wood | Liver, gallbladder | Wind | Sour (酸) | Move, grow, start things |
| **Summer** | Fire | Heart, small intestine | Heat | Bitter (苦) | Peak activity, connection, joy |
| **Late summer** | Earth | Spleen, stomach | Dampness | Sweet (甘) | Ripen, digest, steady the center |
| **Autumn** | Metal | Lungs, large intestine | Dryness | Pungent (辛) | Gather in, let go, refine |
| **Winter** | Water | Kidneys, bladder | Cold | Salty (咸) | Store, rest, conserve |

Read the "focus" column as a verb list and the logic gets clear immediately. The year has a shape: expansion, peak, ripening, contraction, storage. Every other row on the chart is describing the same arc from a different angle.

## Why five seasons, not four

Late summer (长夏, cháng xià) is the one Americans have no name for, and once you've noticed it you can't unnotice it. It's that heavy, humid stretch — roughly August into early September in much of the country — when growth has stopped and things are ripening instead. The air is thick, appetite goes sluggish, and nothing quite dries out.

Chinese medicine assigns that stretch to Earth, the center of the system, and pairs it with dampness and digestion. Anyone who has felt bloated through a humid August already has the empirical version.

There's a second, more elegant version of the same idea. Some classical schemes don't give Earth a season of its own at all. Instead they assign Earth the roughly eighteen-day transition at the end of every season, on the logic that all change has to pass through the center to get anywhere. Both readings are traditional. The second one explains something the first doesn't, which is why the transitions matter so much.

## Season by season, in practice

**Spring / Wood.** Everything moves upward and outward. Traditional advice is to match it: get your body moving daily, sleep a little less and rise earlier, eat young green things and something sour, and — this is the part people skip — deal with what you're irritated about instead of storing it. Wood's emotion is anger, and spring is when stored frustration tends to surface as tension, headaches and poor sleep. Full version: [spring liver care](/blog/spring-liver-care/).

**Summer / Fire.** The year's peak. Be active, be social, be outside. The traditional cautions are specific: protect your sleep even when the light says you don't need it, add a little bitter to clear heat, and don't let a good July turn into a September of overcommitment. Fire's failure mode is not exhaustion in the moment but burning through reserves you'll want later. See [summer heart health](/blog/summer-heart-health-tcm/).

**Late summer / Earth.** The damp middle. Warm cooked food on a regular schedule, less raw and cold, a walk after meals, and a fixed container for worry — Earth's emotion is overthinking, and it circles hardest when the body feels heavy. This is the season where irregular eating costs the most. See [Earth element and digestion](/blog/earth-element-digestion/).

**Autumn / Metal.** The turn inward. Dryness is the season's climate, so moistening food earns its place against autumn dryness (秋燥) — pear, white fungus, lotus root, almonds. Breathe deliberately. And take Metal's grief seriously: autumn is traditionally the season for finishing and releasing things, which is why clearing one drawer genuinely fits the season's logic. See [autumn lung care](/blog/autumn-lung-care/).

**Winter / Water.** Storage. Sleep more, and go to bed earlier than feels productive. Keep your lower back and feet warm. Eat dense, dark, mineral-rich food — black beans (黑豆, hēi dòu), black sesame (黑芝麻, hēi zhī má), seaweed, walnuts, long-simmered soups. The instruction most Americans resist is the actual instruction: doing less in winter is not laziness, it's the assignment. See [winter kidney care](/blog/winter-kidney-care/).

## The handoffs are the risky part

Ask a traditional practitioner when people get sick and you won't hear "in winter." You'll hear "at the change of seasons."

The reasoning follows the generating cycle: each season hands off to the next, and each element is supposed to feed the one that follows. Wood feeds Fire, Fire creates Earth, Earth yields Metal, Metal carries Water, Water grows Wood. A rough handoff — a hot spell in early spring, an abrupt cold snap in late autumn, the collapse of humidity into dry air in September — is where a body that was coping stops coping.

The practical version is unglamorous and works:

- Dress for the transition, not the calendar. The classic mistake is stripping down on the first warm spring day and getting hit by wind.
- Shift your food a week or two *before* the weather does, not a month after.
- Expect an energy dip at every turn; don't schedule your hardest month there.
- If you get sick on the same schedule every year, plan around that date.

The season-to-season handoff is exactly what [the generating cycle](/blog/generating-cycle-five-elements/) describes.

## One habit per season

The chart has fifty boxes. A good year uses five.

- **Spring:** walk outside every day, even briefly.
- **Summer:** hold a real bedtime despite the long light.
- **Late summer:** eat warm and on a schedule for one month.
- **Autumn:** add one moistening food and finish one lingering thing.
- **Winter:** sleep an extra hour and stop apologizing for it.

Every one of those is defensible without invoking any element at all — which is, I'd argue, the strongest thing you can say about a two-thousand-year-old framework.

## What the seasonal chart is not

It's not a calendar you owe compliance to. Late summer in Minnesota and late summer in Florida aren't the same event, and neither matches the North China Plain the system was built on. Read the chart as a pattern — expansion, peak, ripening, contraction, storage — then look out your own window to see where you actually are.

It's also not a substitute for medical care. Seasonal fatigue is common and usually benign; profound seasonal mood change, breathlessness, or fatigue that doesn't lift with rest are reasons to see a physician. The framework tells you when to pay attention, not what's wrong.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| One season of the year is reliably harder on my body than the others | ☐ |
| I get sick at roughly the same time every year | ☐ |
| I eat about the same food in January as I do in July | ☐ |
| I feel heavy and sluggish specifically in humid weather | ☐ |
| I push the same schedule year-round regardless of daylight | ☐ |
| I'd like one habit per season rather than a whole regimen | ☐ |

Three or more checks and seasonal living is the highest-yield place to start with the five elements — more so than any personality reading. If you checked the second one, look at the handoff before that date rather than the season itself.

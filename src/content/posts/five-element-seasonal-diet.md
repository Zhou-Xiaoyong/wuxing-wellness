Most diets are organized around rules: don't eat this, count that, cut out the other thing. The five-element seasonal diet is organized around a wheel. Each season gets an element, an organ, a color, a flavor, and a short list of foods the tradition has been associating with it for over two millennia. You eat with the wheel when you can, ignore it when you can't, and the system is remarkably unbothered by the second part.

This is a food-culture map, not a prescription and not medical advice. Dietary changes for a diagnosed condition, pregnancy, or medications with food interactions belong with a clinician. What follows is the framework as the tradition teaches it, with the honest caveats where they belong.

## How the wheel is built

The five-element system (五行, wǔ xíng) maps one element to each season — including a fifth season most Western calendars skip entirely. The correspondences come from the *Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon* (《黄帝内经》), and every column of the wheel derives from them:

| Season | Element | Organ | Color | Flavor | The kitchen move |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spring | Wood | Liver | Green | Sour (酸) | Fresh, green, rising |
| Summer | Fire | Heart | Red | Bitter (苦) | Cool, light, hydrating |
| Late summer | Earth | Spleen | Yellow | Sweet (甘) | Warm, ground, digestible |
| Autumn | Metal | Lungs | White | Pungent (辛) | Moistening, gentle |
| Winter | Water | Kidneys | Black | Salty (咸) | Warming, storing, slow |

The flavor column is the steering wheel: it's how the tradition nudges an organ toward balance. Each flavor in moderation supports its element; the same flavor in excess is the classical definition of harming it. Sour wakes up Wood, bitter settles Fire, sweet (the grain-and-squash kind, not the candy kind) grounds Earth, pungent moves Metal, salty directs things to Water's depths.

One correction the tradition itself makes: the Sweet (甘) that grounds Earth means rice, oats, squash, carrots, dates — the mild staples. It does not mean dessert. Western readers do the system no favors by translating gan (甘) as "sugar."

## Spring — Wood: the green season

Spring's energy rises, and the food follows: young greens, sprouts, scallions, and sour accents like lemon, vinegar, and hawthorn that the tradition uses to wake the liver after winter's heaviness. Cooking gets lighter — quick stir-fries and blanching replace the braises. The complete plate, with a three-day template, is in [what to eat in spring](/blog/what-to-eat-in-spring-tcm/).

**Reach for:** spinach, dandelion greens, sprouts, leeks, lemon, plum, vinegar-dressed anything.

## Summer — Fire: the cooling season

Summer rules the heart, and its food problem is excess heat. The tradition's answer is cool *foods* served warm: mung beans simmered into soup, watermelon, cucumber, bitter greens, lotus seed, mint. The counterintuitive rule — less ice, more light cooking — trips up every Western reader once, then makes sense forever. The full argument is in [summer cooling foods](/blog/summer-cooling-foods-chinese/).

**Reach for:** mung beans, watermelon, cucumber, leafy bitters, lotus seed, mint, chamomile.

## Late summer — Earth: the season nobody has

The humid weeks straddling August and September are Earth's own season, ruled by the spleen and stomach — digestion itself. The food is deliberately dull: congee, millet, squash, sweet potato, carrots. Warm, soft, mildly sweet, boring on purpose, so an overworked digestive system can reset after summer's iced drinks and irregular meals. This is the season most diets miss entirely; our [late summer & spleen care](/blog/late-summer-spleen-care/) piece is the deep dive.

**Reach for:** rice congee, millet, pumpkin and squash, sweet potato, corn, dates (红枣, hóng zǎo).

## Autumn — Metal: the moistening season

Autumn is dry, Metal rules the lungs, and the color correspondence says white. Hence the most elegant list on the wheel: pear, snow fungus, lily bulb, lotus root, white radish, honey — foods chosen for a silky, moistening quality that plain water doesn't deliver. Pungent shows up in small amounts to help the lungs disperse. The complete white-foods list is in [autumn foods for the lungs](/blog/autumn-foods-for-lungs/).

**Reach for:** pear, snow fungus (银耳, yín ěr), lily bulb, lotus root, daikon, almonds, honey.

## Winter — Water: the warming season

Winter is storage, the kidneys, and heat. The wheel swings to black foods and slow, deep cooking: black beans, black sesame, walnuts, dark poultry, lamb, ginger, cinnamon, and broths that simmer for hours. Salty appears as seasoning, not strategy — the classical caution is that excess salt taxes the kidneys it's meant to support. The warming pantry in detail is in [winter warming foods](/blog/winter-warming-foods-tcm/).

**Reach for:** black beans, walnuts, black sesame, lamb, ginger, cinnamon, long-cooked soups.

## Running the wheel in real life

Three rules keep this from becoming a part-time job. First, the staples do the work — change the center of the plate seasonally and let everything else be normal food. Second, transitions beat seasons: the weeks around each solstice and equinox are when the tradition shifts the kitchen, so change yours there rather than waiting for weather to force it. Third, the wheel is a compass, not a cage — a salad in January or a hot pot in July is a rounding error, not a failure.

## The honest limit

The correspondences are a two-thousand-year-old pattern language, not clinical data. Where the wheel genuinely earns its keep is that it encodes good instincts most modern advice reaches separately: eat with the local harvest, cook more when it's cold, hydrate with structure when it's dry, keep the diet boring for a few weeks when your digestion is overwhelmed. The tradition just attaches a memorable column of colors and flavors to each one.

Anything medical stays with a clinician. The wheel just decides what's for dinner — five seasons a year.

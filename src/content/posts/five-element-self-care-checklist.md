The bathroom counter in the first week of November holds four bottles, a tub of magnesium powder with the scoop still buried in it, and a humidifier that has been empty since Sunday. You bought all of it in the last six weeks. If one of them disappeared tonight, the only absence you would feel is the humidifier's.

Everything on that counter is an addition. That is how wellness is sold, and it is only half of how the tradition works. The other half is subtraction, and subtraction is what a checklist can actually carry, because a stop list is short by nature.

What follows is five things to stop, one per element, sized to fit on a single index card. There is nothing to fill in and nothing to score. It is not medical advice.

## The half of the prescription that gets skipped

In the chapter on the three-part pulse and the nine indicators (《素问·三部九候论》), the physician is told to weigh the patient's build, judge whether the qi is replete or deficient, and then act: drain the replete, supplement the deficient (实则泻之，虚则补之). Two verbs, equal weight. Western shelves stock only the second one.

The catalog of verbs is longer, and most of it is removal. In the chapter on the grand principles of treatment (《素问·至真要大论》), a binding is dispersed (结者散之), dryness is moistened (燥者濡之), urgency is slackened (急者缓之), and a hardness is shaved down (坚者削之). Four of the canon's moves are things you take away.

There is also a ceiling on how far to go, and it is stricter than anything printed on a bottle. In the chapter on the five constants and the great policies (《素问·五常政大论》), a strong remedy stops at six parts in ten, a moderate one at seven, a mild one at eight, and even a harmless one at nine. Food finishes the rest, and the line closes with a warning: do not overshoot, or you injure the upright qi (无使过之，伤其正也).

Six tenths. That is the canon's limit for an intervention, which makes five of them stacked on a counter look like what it is.

## Five lines on a four-by-six card

The format does half the work. A checklist that fits on an index card gets read, because it can be taped inside a cabinet door. One that runs to a page gets filed.

A 4 × 6 card with a quarter-inch margin leaves 3.5 inches of width. At 10-point type that is about 38 characters to a line, and an element column eats a third of it, so each entry gets roughly 25 characters. Five rows at three lines, plus a header and the four verbs, lands inside 5.5 inches of height. The constraint is what keeps the entries honest. "Reduce your caffeine intake" does not fit on the card. "No coffee after 2 p.m." does.

| Element | Stop | You will know it is yours if | Keep |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wood | The hard workout you use as a pressure valve | Your jaw is tight by mid-morning and you sigh without noticing | A 20-minute walk, no pace target |
| Fire | Coffee after 2 p.m. | You fall asleep fine and wake at 3 | The first cup, sitting down |
| Earth | The lunch you eat standing up | You are hungry again ninety minutes later | Twenty minutes at a table |
| Metal | The long hot shower and the decongestant spray | Your throat is dry by morning and the spray lives in your bag | The humidifier, actually filled |
| Water | Sweating on purpose in the cold months | The sauna leaves you flatter than it found you | Sleep, and the dark before it |

Column four is not decoration. A subtraction with nothing behind it leaves a hole, and a hole gets filled with the next thing on the shelf.

## Three of these have a number attached

Caffeine clears at a half-life of about five hours in a healthy adult, with a range of three to seven depending on genetics and smoking. A cup at 2 p.m. leaves roughly a third of its dose in you at ten at night, and a quarter at midnight. Half of the sleep problem people describe as insomnia is arithmetic, and arithmetic is fixable.

The spray is the line with a hard limit. Oxymetazoline, the active ingredient in most over-the-counter decongestant sprays, produces rebound congestion after about three consecutive days: the tissue swells back harder each time a dose wears off. It is the one item on the card where the thing to stop is a product that genuinely worked.

Water's prohibition is the oldest. Winter in the four-seasons chapter is described as closing and storing (冬三月，此谓闭藏), with a single instruction attached to the season: do not disturb the yang (无扰乎阳). Sweat shows why. The chapter on the five qi of clarity (《素问·宣明五气篇》) gives each organ a fluid, and the heart's is sweat (心为汗). A hot yoga class in July is a fair exchange. The same class in January spends a reserve the season is asking you to bank, which is why the tradition's winter exercise looks so dull. Walk. Move. Do not pour.

Wood's line has a modern companion. Cyclic sighing, a double inhale through the nose followed by a long exhale through the mouth, was tested against mindfulness meditation in a 2023 Stanford trial: five minutes a day for four weeks, and the sighing group improved mood and lowered resting breathing rate slightly more than the meditators did. The mechanism is unglamorous. A long exhale offloads carbon dioxide and puts a hand on the parasympathetic brake. If the workout is the only place your tension gets addressed, the exhale is free and the workout is not.

## One at a time, no log

The card has no boxes. Every other checklist in this section asks you to record something; this one asks you to read a column, pick a line, and cross it out.

Two weeks is long enough. Not because fourteen is a magic number, but because it covers two full work cycles, which is where most habits announce themselves.

The test is one question asked twice, and it is not a journal entry. On the last Tuesday of the two weeks, ask whether this Tuesday was better than the Tuesday before you started. Compare a weekday to a weekday, not to a vacation. If you cannot tell, the answer is no, and that is worth knowing. Most of a supplement shelf fails that question, which is why nobody asks it.

The six-tenths rule applies to habits as well as herbs. You are not trying to reach zero coffees or zero hard workouts. You are trying to find the point where the pattern stops being the thing that runs your week.

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I have added something to my routine this year and removed nothing | ☐ |
| My first answer to feeling bad is usually a purchase | ☐ |
| I have used a decongestant spray for more than three days running | ☐ |
| I cannot say what my last health change actually did | ☐ |
| I would stop one thing for two weeks if someone named it | ☐ |

Three or more, and the card is the right format. If only the last row is true, you already know which line to take; the rest of the page is just permission.

## Before Saturday

Beginning of Winter lands this Saturday, which makes the Water line the seasonal one. Take it first: pick the one deliberate sweat session in your week and move it to a walk, or shorten it by half. Then tape the card inside the cabinet where the mugs live, because a card in a drawer is a card you have already stopped reading.

Two caveats, and they are not boilerplate. Fatigue that arrives with breathlessness, chest tightness, palpitations, or weight loss is not a seasonal pattern, and no line on this card is a substitute for having it looked at. And nothing here is a reason to stop a medication you were prescribed, including the spray, which is worth a conversation with a pharmacist if you have been reaching for it for a month.

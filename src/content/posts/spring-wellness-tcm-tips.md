There's a reason "spring cleaning" is a phrase in every culture. Something in us wants to open the windows, move the stale air, and start again. Chinese medicine has a structured version of that instinct, and it's tied to a specific season-and-organ pairing: spring is Wood, and Wood's organ is the liver (肝, gān).

This is a cultural and habit guide, not medical advice. If you have a diagnosed liver condition or a digestive disorder your doctor is managing, keep that plan in place and treat the ideas below as flavor and rhythm, not protocol.

## The one idea behind all the tips

In five-element theory, Wood is the phase of rising, spreading, outward movement — seeds cracking, buds forcing open, energy pushing up. The liver is the organ that keeps that energy flowing smoothly rather than pooling or backing up. Spring is when the outward pressure is at its most insistent, so the whole season's advice points the same direction: support the rise, don't obstruct it. Every tip below is a version of that one sentence.

## Sleep on the season's clock

The classical text, the Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon (《黄帝内经》), gives the most specific seasonal sleep instruction of the whole year for spring: retire early, rise early, and "walk in the courtyard with your hair loose." The point isn't the hairstyle — it's unhurried, open, outward movement first thing. The liver's window on the body clock runs 1 to 3 a.m. People who sleep well before midnight tend to feel the season's energy as vitality; people who don't tend to feel it as the wired-and-irritable version. No food fixes a sleep debt, so this comes first.

## Move, specifically outdoors

Wood rules the tendons and the free flow of energy, so movement is the single most reliable spring tool. A daily walk in fresh air, ideally with some green around you, does more for a stalled spring mood than an hour of analysis. Add gentle stretching, especially side bends and twists — the liver channel runs up the flanks, and side-opening stretches are the traditional release for Wood tension. You don't need a program; you need a door.

## Watch the temper, gently

The emotion assigned to Wood is anger (怒, nù) — but the slow pressure of frustration, not the loud kind. Spring's rising energy, when it has nowhere to go, turns inward as jaw-clench and a short fuse. The tradition's advice isn't "calm down." It's give the energy a channel: a walk, a stretch, a conversation you've been avoiding. Noticing the pattern is most of the work.

## Feed the eyes

In this system the liver "opens to the eyes," so eye fatigue is read as a downstream liver signal. Spring is when screens have usually done their winter damage, and the traditional move is the obvious one: rest the eyes, look at distance, and add the liver-and-eyes food the whole tradition agrees on — goji berries (枸杞, gǒu qǐ), a small handful in tea or congee. Nothing dramatic happens; that's the point. It's a daily-habit food, not an intervention.

## Put the right food on the plate

The shift is easier to feel than to memorize:

- **Go younger and greener.** Sprouts, shoots, garlic chives (韭菜, jiǔ cài), tender greens, the first asparagus — foods literally in their growth stage.
- **Cook it quick.** Stir-fry, blanch, steam. Less long braising and roasting than you ate in January.
- **A little pungency, not a lot of sour.** The Inner Canon says the liver is helped by gentle Pungent (辛) and tightened by excess Sour (酸). So scallion, chives, mint, a little ginger — and go easy on heavily pickled food.
- **Ease off the heavy.** Fried, greasy, very rich food is the season's most consistent caution.
- **Add one mild sweet counterweight.** Chinese yam (山药, shān yào), jujube dates (红枣, hóng zǎo), millet — a gently nourishing food so the sharper spring foods don't leave you scraped out.

## A weekly version you can actually keep

Three levers capture most of the value. First, one green vegetable cooked quickly, every day. Second, one aromatic finish daily — chives, cilantro, a little grated ginger at the end of a dish. Third, one genuinely outdoor movement session, even if it's a ten-minute walk. Run those for two weeks and notice your mood in the late afternoon rather than anything you can measure. That's the honest scale for this kind of practice.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I feel more energetic and hopeful when spring arrives | ☐ |
| My mood resets faster after I get outside and move | ☐ |
| My eyes feel tired or dry by the end of most days | ☐ |
| I get irritable when plans stall, more than I'd like | ☐ |
| Heavy, fried meals sit worse on me in spring than winter | ☐ |
| I sleep better when I'm in bed before midnight | ☐ |

Mostly checks means the Wood-spring rhythm is already working for you — keep it. If the irritable and tired rows dominate, the food and movement shift is worth a real try before anything else.

## The honest boundary

None of these tips treat a condition. What they do is give you a coherent answer to "what should I do this season," built on a couple of millennia of observation and gentle enough that being wrong about it costs you nothing. Persistent fatigue, upper-right abdominal pain, yellowing of the eyes or skin, or dark urine belong with a clinician, not a teapot.

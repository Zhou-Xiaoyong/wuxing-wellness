Day three is where downloaded meal plans die. Not day one, when the fridge is stocked and everything is chopped. Day three, 6:20 on a Wednesday, when the plan's page for today says braised daikon and you have forty minutes and no daikon.

The pdf was never the problem. The problem is that a food list is not a plan. A week holds twenty-eight meal slots, and "eat more bitter greens in summer" has nothing to say about slot twenty-two. So the plan below is a grid with slots in it, and the food is downstream of that.

What follows is a traditional framework used to decide what to cook. It is not medical advice, and it is not a therapeutic diet. Nothing here diagnoses anything.

## The classical ratio almost nobody quotes

The line worth carrying into a kitchen is from the *Plain Questions* (《素问·脏气法时论》): grains for nourishment, fruit as support, meat as benefit, vegetables as fill (五谷为养，五果为助，五畜为益，五菜为充).

Read the order. Grain is the foundation, not the thing you subtract. Fruit assists rather than stars. Meat enriches. Vegetables supply bulk. That is a plate with a grain base and a vegetable volume, which is close to the inverse of the low-carb plate that dominated American diet advice for twenty years, and it is also close to what most of the world eats by default.

The same chapter ties the five flavors to the five organs, and that is where the plate rule comes from. Sour (酸) gathers, bitter (苦) drains, sweet (甘) slows and steadies, pungent (辛) disperses, salty (咸) softens. You do not need all five at every meal. You need more than two across a day.

## The four-part slot

Every slot on the grid is built from the same four parts. Learn the rule and you can throw the pdf away.

**Base grain, a cupped hand.** About half a cup cooked. One cup of dry rice makes three cups cooked, which is why a five-pound bag runs a single person close to three weeks at a cup and a half a day.

**Cooked vegetables, two cupped hands.** Cooked, not a pile of cold leaves. The tradition's reasoning is that the stomach and spleen handle warmed, softened fiber more easily, and there is a plain nutrition case for it too: heat breaks cell walls, so cooked carrots give up more beta-carotene and cooked tomatoes more lycopene than the raw versions.

**Protein, a palm.** Three to four ounces. Chicken thigh, fish, tofu, eggs, adzuki beans.

**One flavor that steers the meal.** A splash of black vinegar on greens. A bitter green blanched thirty seconds. Ginger and scallion in the oil before anything else goes in. This is the part American cooking drops, and it is the part that decides whether the same three ingredients taste like two different dinners.

Then one more thing per day, outside the grid: a warm drink, taken without a task attached to it.

## One week, late October

Late October sits on the seam between Metal and Earth, dry air over a harvest. The week below leans on pears, daikon, shiitake, millet and winter squash, and it is built on a two-day cycle: tonight's dinner is tomorrow's lunch. Seven dinners and seven breakfasts means fourteen cooking sessions for twenty-eight slots, not twenty-one.

| Day | Breakfast | Lunch | Dinner | Warm drink |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Millet porridge (小米粥, xiǎo mǐ zhōu) with diced pear and toasted walnuts | Rice with shiitake and bok choy, sesame oil | Poached chicken thighs with daikon and ginger | Warm water, plain |
| Tue | Congee with red date (红枣, hóng zǎo) and three slices of ginger | Monday's chicken and daikon, reheated with fresh scallion | Steamed egg custard over rice, side of squash | Pear and chrysanthemum tea |
| Wed | Oat and millet porridge, a spoon of black sesame | Tuesday's egg custard, cold rice turned into fried rice | Braised tofu with shiitake and carrot | Warm water with a slice of lemon |
| Thu | Congee with a spoon of adzuki beans | Wednesday's tofu and mushrooms | Baked white fish, steamed Napa cabbage, rice | Toasted barley tea |
| Fri | Millet porridge with pear again, because it works | Thursday's fish flaked through rice with ginger | Chicken and daikon soup, second pot of the week | Pear tea |
| Sat | Two eggs, leftover rice, scallion | Friday's soup | Hand-pulled noodles in the soup broth, greens on top | Warm water |
| Sun | Congee, plain, with pickled mustard green | Whatever is left, reassembled | A pot of something new, cooked double | Barley tea |

Two rules make that table hold. Cook the grain in a three-cup batch at the start of the week, since three cups dry become nine cups cooked and reheat in ninety seconds. And poach the chicken in one session at a bare simmer for twelve minutes, to 165°F, then use it three ways: sliced hot on Monday, shredded cold on Tuesday, returned to the pot on Friday.

Day-old rice is not a compromise. Cooling cooked rice converts part of its starch to resistant starch, which studies put at roughly one and a half to two times the fresh level, and reheating does not undo it.

## Twelve items, twenty-eight slots

The grocery list is short because the grid repeats its components. Five pounds of short-grain rice. One bag of millet. A pound of adzuki beans. Two pounds of chicken thighs. One pound of firm tofu. A white fish fillet. A daikon. A head of Napa cabbage. A pound of bok choy. Fresh ginger and scallions. Dried shiitake. Pears and one winter squash.

That is a cart, not a project. The batch work is two hours on a Sunday: three cups of rice in the cooker, one pot of congee base, one sheet pan of squash at 425°F for twenty-five minutes, chicken poached and cooled. The shiitake soaking water goes into the rice pot instead of the sink, because that is where the savoriness is.

Fiber is the one number worth glancing at, at 25 to 30 grams a day for most adults. Half a cup of cooked adzuki beans carries about eight grams on its own, which is why they appear in the breakfast column rather than as a side dish.

## What the plan leaves out on purpose

No calorie targets and no macro split. Nobody keeps a spreadsheet past week two, and the four-part plate settles portion size without arithmetic.

No five-day reset, no liver flush, no "detox week." The argument is worth repeating: the liver detoxifies continuously, and a week of juice does not improve on that.

No supplement slot. No elimination of a food group unless a clinician has told you to eliminate it.

One honest limit on the whole exercise: this is a plan for a person, not for a condition. If you are managing chronic kidney disease, diabetes on insulin, or a thyroid condition on medication, the potassium, protein, or iodine load of any week's menu is a conversation with your doctor, not a printable.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I've saved a meal plan before and quit inside a week | ☐ |
| My dinners get decided at 6 p.m., not the night before | ☐ |
| I eat lunch out more than three days a week | ☐ |
| I own a pot or cooker I trust with grains | ☐ |
| I'd rather cook one pot twice than two fresh meals | ☐ |
| I skip breakfast and then eat a large dinner | ☐ |

Four or more and the grid is the fix, not a new shopping list. If only the second one is true, start by writing down tomorrow's dinner tonight and leave the rest alone.

## Start with Wednesday

Print the grid and fill in Wednesday first. It is the day that decides whether the other six survive, and it is the day most plans leave blank.

Then cook the rice and poach the chicken, and let the week be ordinary. If a symptom is what sent you looking for a diet plan in the first place, take the sheet to your doctor before you change your plate around it. Food is the part of this you control. Diagnosis is not.

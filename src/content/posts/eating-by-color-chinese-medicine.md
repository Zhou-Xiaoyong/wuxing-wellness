If you walk through a Chinese market with this framework in mind, the produce map starts to read like a chart. Green leafy tops in spring, red dates and goji in the dried-goods aisle, yellow millet and squash in the grain bins, pale pear and lotus root in autumn, black beans and sesame in the winter stack. Chinese food therapy (食疗, shí liáo) organizes a lot of that everyday instinct into one tidy row: five colors, each tied to an element and an organ system. This piece is the how-to — the conceptual map lives in [Eating by Color in Chinese Medicine](/blog/five-colors-food-chinese-medicine/).

This is a food-and-culture guide, not medical advice. No color of food treats any condition, and the tradition itself frames color as daily nourishment, never as intervention. If you notice a persistent change in your own complexion, that is a reason to see a clinician, not to consult a chart.

## The five-color row, in plain terms

Green (青) → Wood / liver; red (赤) → Fire / heart; yellow (黄) → Earth / spleen; white (白) → Metal / lungs; black (黑) → Water / kidneys. The row is a shopping and cooking heuristic, not a mechanism. A black bean is a good food; it is not a kidney treatment. The real value is that it makes "eat a varied diet" concrete and memorable — the same job Western "eat the rainbow" advice does, via a different route.

## Start with one color per day

You don't need all five at once. The simplest habit: add one deliberately missing color to a meal you already eat.

- **Green (Wood):** a handful of greens or sprouts with lunch — celery, broccoli, fresh herbs.
- **Red (Fire):** red dates (红枣, hóng zǎo) or goji berries (枸杞, gǒu qǐ) stirred into oatmeal or rice; tomato or beet alongside.
- **Yellow (Earth):** millet, squash, or sweet potato as the base — the centering, easily digested category.
- **White (Metal):** pear, white fungus (雪耳, xuě ěr), lotus root (莲藕, lián ǒu), or daikon; the moistening foods you reach for against autumn dryness (秋燥).
- **Black (Water):** black beans (黑豆, hēi dòu), black sesame (黑芝麻, hēi zhī má), walnuts; the dense winter category.

The American plate tends to collapse into beige-and-green — grains and lettuce, little red, white, or black. One intentional addition a day closes that gap faster than a full pantry overhaul.

## Pair color with flavor

Color tells you what to shop; flavor tells you what the food does. They're two different rows. Red goji berries are Fire-red and gently sweet (Earth-friendly), which is partly why they show up in so many mild tonics. Green food is usually Wood, but its flavor is sour only sometimes — bitter greens and sweet squash are both green and not sour. Use the two together: [the five flavors](/blog/five-flavors-balancing/) cover the flavor half; color is the shopping twin.

## A sample color week

- **Mon:** green + yellow — stir-fried greens over millet.
- **Tue:** red + white — goji in oatmeal, pear in the afternoon.
- **Wed:** black + green — black-bean bowl with broccoli.
- **Thu:** white + yellow — lotus-root soup with squash.
- **Fri:** red + black — adzuki beans (红豆, hóng dòu) and a few walnuts.
- **Weekend:** all five in one pot — rice, greens, goji, squash, lotus, black bean.

Notice the palette widens across the week without forcing a monochrome meal.

## Working with the seasons

The color row also tracks the calendar. Spring leans green (Wood, upward and fresh); summer leans red (Fire, warming and blood-nourishing); late summer leans yellow (Earth, centering in humidity); autumn leans white (Metal, moistening against dryness); winter leans black (Water, dense and restorative). You don't have to follow it rigidly — that would turn dinner into a chore — but letting the season nudge your shopping keeps the habit effortless. Eat the squash that's cheap in October; reach for greens when they're abundant in May.

One practical note: frozen and dried foods count. Frozen blueberries and edamame carry their color; dried goji and black bean keep theirs. You don't need a farmers-market run to widen the palette — the pantry and freezer do most of the work.

## When color is not medicine

Two honest boundaries keep this useful. First, a food's color organizes a shopping list; it does not establish a mechanism, and it never substitutes for a clinician. A persistent, unexplained change in your complexion is a reason to make an appointment, not consult a chart. Second, color in your home is environmental and psychological, not physiological — paint a bedroom Water-black if it helps you sleep, but don't expect the wall to reach your kidneys. The cultural logic behind the row is spelled out in [the five elements color meaning](/blog/five-elements-color-meaning/).

| Statement | Feels like me |

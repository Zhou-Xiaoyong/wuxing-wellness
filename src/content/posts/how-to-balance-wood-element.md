By four in the afternoon the pressure has settled behind your right eye. Your shoulders are up near your ears, the breath you take stops around the collarbone, and dinner sits in you like a stone in a pocket. Nothing is wrong enough to mention to anyone, and it has been building since Tuesday.

Most people read that as stress and then treat it as one problem. Five-element theory reads it as one pattern with three outlets. Wood, the phase that governs expansion and forward motion, has more push in it than the body is currently able to check. The tradition has a word for the checking, and it is not "relax."

What follows is a traditional framework for reading that pattern and a few habits built around it. It is not medical advice, it does not diagnose anything, and it is not a substitute for a clinician. Persistent headaches, new digestive trouble, or a mood that has gone flat for weeks belong in an exam room.

## Surplus is a different animal from stuck

Most liver advice available in English assumes one direction. The qi is stuck, so you move it: walk, stretch, add something sour, get things flowing. That is the right answer for a system that has stalled. It is the wrong answer for a system that is already moving hard.

The distinction is old and fairly blunt. In the chapter on the five movements (《素问·五运行大论》), the canon describes what excess does inside the control cycle: qi in surplus over-controls what it would normally govern and rides over what would normally govern it (气有余，则制己所胜而侮所不胜). Applied to Wood, both halves show up in the same week. Over-control lands on Earth, which is the spleen and stomach, so meals sit heavily on your worst days. The insult lands on Metal, which is the lungs, so the breath goes shallow and you keep sighing to finish it.

| | Wood that has stalled | Wood that has overshot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it feels like | Flat, braced, unable to start | Wired, tight, unable to stop |
| What usually helps | Movement, aromatics, a plan | Descent, sour, one fewer commitment |
| What backfires | Rest and waiting | More intensity, more plans, more yes |

Both patterns can produce a short temper and a tight jaw, which is why the two get confused. The tell is direction: one wants to get going, the other cannot come down.

## The brake sits on the other side of the cycle

The chapter on the six subtle principles (《素问·六微旨大论》) puts the rule in six characters: when something runs to excess it harms, and what receives it restrains it (亢则害，承乃制). Restraint is not imposed from outside. It is a property of the thing downstream.

In the five-phase cycle, Metal checks Wood. That is easy to read as a metaphor about discipline, and the tradition means something more physical. Metal's movement is descending and clarifying, the lungs' work of sending things down and out. Wood's movement is rising and spreading. A body carrying surplus Wood has a great deal of up and not much down, and the correction is to restore the down.

Which is why "calm down" fails. There is no lever in it. There are three levers, and none of them require you to feel differently.

## Lever one: lengthen the out-breath, on a count

Inhalation is muscular work, driven by the diaphragm. At rest, exhalation is mostly elastic recoil, and it is the phase wired to the parasympathetic side of the nervous system. You cannot slow your heartbeat on command. You can slow the out-breath, and the rest of the cycle tends to follow.

The traditional instruction, stripped of ceremony: sit upright, breathe in through the nose for a count of four, out through softly pursed lips for a count of eight, six times. Twice a day. That cycle runs twelve seconds, five breaths a minute, and five a minute happens to sit inside the band where heart rate variability peaks in laboratory work. Herbert Benson's group and later Paul Lehrer's resonant-frequency studies put most adults between roughly 4.5 and 6.5 breaths per minute. Going slower than that is not more effective. It mostly makes people drowsy.

One more thing about the sigh. A spontaneous sigh fires every few minutes and re-inflates air sacs that have partly collapsed, a reflex traced in 2016 to a small cluster of neurons in the brainstem. It is housekeeping, not despair. Somebody sighing audibly ten times an hour is not relaxed. The housekeeping has stopped holding.

## Lever two: sour holds what pungent moves

Here is where the popular advice sorts wrong. The chapter on the qi of the organs and the seasons (《素问·脏气法时论》) says the liver wants to spread, and when it urgently needs to, use pungent to spread it. Then it adds the part people skip: pungent is the liver's tonic, and sour is its drain (肝欲散，急食辛以散之。用辛补之，酸泻之).

Pungent earns "tonic" because it restores the organ's own motion; sour earns "drain" because astringing stops an overshoot. So the flavor depends on which of the two columns above you are in. Stuck Wood wants mint, rose bud, a slice of fresh ginger. Wood that has overshot wants sour in food amounts: a tablespoon of vinegar in a dressing, one sour plum, lemon peel rather than a quart of lemon water. The all-day lemon habit is the popular version of a good idea applied to the wrong pattern.

Bitter is the third lever, and it handles the heat that rides on surplus Wood, the red eyes and the temple throbbing. Arugula, dandelion greens, chrysanthemum in a cup. One bitter vegetable a day is a dose. A bitter supplement is a different thing.

For descent specifically, the shopping list is short and unglamorous. Pear, daikon radish, almond, cooked apple. Metal-side foods, and [Pungent Foods and the Lungs](/blog/pungent-foods-lungs-chinese-medicine/) explains why a small amount goes further than a large one.

## Lever three: one boundary a week

Metal also governs the boundary. The skin is the literal version. "No" is the social one, and it is the lever surplus Wood resists most, because expansion is what surplus Wood does: another project, another yes, another plan layered on a calendar that was already full.

One refusal a week, spoken out loud, with no justification attached. Name it on Sunday and say it by Wednesday. If the full no is impossible this week, the smaller version still works: not this week.

## Two weeks, three levers

- **Twice a day:** six rounds of four in, eight out, seated, before breakfast and before dinner.
- **Once a day:** one sour or bitter element at the meal where you feel tightest.
- **Once a week:** one audible no, no explanation.
- **Daily:** twenty minutes of walking with the exhale longer than the inhale. Not a workout.

Skip the pot of cooling tea, the liver cleanse, and the extra hard session as penance. That last one is usually the surplus talking, and it adds up without ever adding down.

Start with the breath, because it is the only one of the three you can pull in a meeting without anyone noticing. Two weeks is enough to know whether the pattern is responding. If the temple headaches arrive with visual changes, if the digestive trouble is new and constant, or if it wakes you at night, that is a clinician's question and not a five-element one.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure or throbbing settles behind one eye or at the temples | ☐ |
| My breath stops high in my chest and I sigh to finish it | ☐ |
| Meals sit heavily on the days I push hardest | ☐ |
| I have added things to fix this and ended up tighter | ☐ |
| My shoulders and jaw are the first place tension lands | ☐ |
| I say yes before I check the calendar | ☐ |

Four or more checks, and the pattern is surplus rather than stalled. Two or fewer, and the softer routine in the links below is probably the better fit.

If you've seen a five elements chart before, you've seen organ names in it — Liver under Wood, Heart under Fire, and so on down the line. What most charts don't tell you is that each element actually claims *two* organs, that the pairing is deliberate, and that the organ names mean something quite different from what your anatomy textbook says.

That gap causes a lot of confusion. Someone reads that the "spleen" governs digestion, checks a Western source, discovers the spleen is a lymphatic organ that filters blood, and reasonably concludes the whole system is nonsense. It isn't nonsense — it's a different naming convention, roughly two thousand years older, describing functional systems rather than pieces of tissue.

This is the organ-by-organ version of the chart, with the pairings, what each system is said to govern, and where it shows up on the surface of you.

Standard caveat first: this is a traditional framework for organizing observation, not a diagnostic tool. Nothing here is medical advice, and a chart is not a substitute for a clinician who can actually examine you.

Five rows, and every column in it is doing work. Let me take them one at a time.

## Zang and fu: why the organs come in pairs

The split is the most useful idea on the chart, and it's usually skipped.

The **zang** organs are yin: solid, dense, and in the traditional description they *store*. Liver, heart, spleen, lungs, kidneys. They hold the valuable stuff — blood, qi, essence — and they work quietly and continuously.

The **fu** organs are yang: hollow, and they *transform and move*. Gallbladder, small intestine, stomach, large intestine, bladder. They receive something, do something to it, and pass it along. Nothing is supposed to sit still in a fu organ.

Each element gets one of each, and the pair is understood as a single system with two jobs — a reservoir and a pipeline. Wood stores strategy in the liver and executes decisions through the gallbladder. Metal takes in through the lungs and lets go through the large intestine. Once you see that rhythm — hold and release, receive and transform — the pairings stop looking arbitrary.

## What each element's organ system actually governs

**Wood — Liver and Gallbladder.** The liver's headline job is the smooth flow of qi through the whole body. When flow is easy, mood is even and things move on schedule; when it's stuck, the tradition expects irritability, tension across the ribs and shoulders, and a short fuse. The liver also stores blood and is said to open into the eyes, which is why tired, gritty eyes get read as a Wood sign. The gallbladder supplies decisiveness — in classical language, the courage to actually choose.

**Fire — Heart and Small Intestine.** The heart governs blood, but more importantly it houses the shen (神, shén), usually translated as spirit or consciousness. That makes the heart the seat of sleep quality, mental clarity and emotional warmth, not just circulation. It opens into the tongue, so speech that races or falters counts as information. The small intestine's job is sorting: separating what's useful from what isn't, both in food and, by analogy, in judgment.

**Earth — Spleen and Stomach.** This pair is the digestive engine, and in Chinese medicine it's the source of postnatal qi — everything you build after birth from food and air. The stomach receives and "rots and ripens" what you eat; the spleen transforms it and sends the good part upward. Earth governs the muscles and opens into the mouth, which is why poor appetite, heavy limbs and loose stools cluster together in this framework.

**Metal — Lungs and Large Intestine.** The lungs govern qi and the exterior — including the skin and the defensive layer the tradition calls wei qi (卫气, wèi qì). They dislike dryness, which is why autumn dryness (秋燥) hits this system first. The large intestine handles release, and the pairing is not just physical: Metal is the element of grief, and both letting go emotionally and letting go physically live in the same column.

**Water — Kidneys and Bladder.** The kidneys store jing (精, jīng), essence — the constitutional battery you're born with and slowly spend. They govern bones, teeth, hearing and reproduction, and they anchor the will. The bladder handles the fluid endpoint. Water is the deepest system in the chart and the slowest to rebuild, which is why the traditional advice for it is almost always rest rather than stimulation.

## How to actually use the body clock column

The two-hour windows are the part readers find most striking, and the part most easily over-read.

The claim is that qi peaks in each organ system for two hours a day. In practice the only rows most people can use are the sleep ones. Waking reliably at 2 a.m. lands in the liver window and gets read as a Wood pattern — often stress, alcohol, or a late heavy dinner. A 4 a.m. wake with a dry throat lands in the lung window. An energy crash at 3 p.m. sits at the start of the bladder–kidney stretch and usually means you slept badly, not that your kidneys are failing.

Use it as a prompt to notice timing. Do not use it to diagnose yourself at 2 a.m., which is the worst possible hour for accurate self-assessment anyway.

## Reading the chart without overreading it

Three rules keep this useful.

**Organ names are categories, not anatomy.** The Chinese medicine "kidney" includes hearing, bone density and libido. The "spleen" includes appetite and muscle tone. Translate every organ word in your head as "the *system* traditionally called…" and most of the friction disappears.

**Look for clusters.** One symptom in a row means nothing. Dry skin plus a dry cough plus constipation, all in October, is a Metal-column cluster — and clusters are what this chart is genuinely good at spotting.

**The pairs move together.** If you're working on Earth, you don't treat "the spleen" in isolation; you change how and when you eat, which is stomach territory. The system was designed to be addressed as a pair.

The correspondences here follow the mainstream reading laid out in the Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon (《黄帝内经》) and elaborated over the following centuries. They were never perfectly uniform across schools, and anyone presenting them as one settled doctrine is smoothing over real historical variation.

A Western reader hears "eat your greens" and thinks vitamins. Chinese medicine hears the same advice through a different lens: green is the color of Wood, and Wood's organ is the liver, so the daily pile of greens is also a way to keep the season's rising energy moving. This is a food-and-culture guide, not medical advice — none of it treats liver disease. If you have hepatitis, fatty liver, elevated enzymes, or you're on medication your doctor monitors, keep your clinician in the loop; the food ideas here are seasoning on top of that plan, never a substitute for it.

Green sits at one corner of the five-color food map. The tradition reads green as young, fresh, and upward-moving — the quality of spring sprouts forcing through soil — which is exactly the quality it wants the liver to embody: flow, not stagnation. That is the logic linking the color to the organ, and it is why spring is the season green foods get their loudest endorsement.

## Why green maps to Wood and the liver

In five-element theory, spring belongs to Wood, and Wood's organ is the liver. The link is not anatomical; it is about motion. Wood is the phase of rising, spreading, pushing outward. The liver in this system is the organ that keeps energy flowing smoothly — plans, emotion, digestion, blood distribution. Green food, caught mid-growth, is the edible version of that upward push.

Not every green food is Wood's friend in the same way. The tradition distinguishes young, fresh greens from bitter ones and from sweet ones, and the flavor you add changes the effect. A little mild Sweet (甘) softens Wood's tension; a little Sour (酸) gathers it; bitter greens cool rather than move. Color tells you the family; flavor tells you the specific job.

## The green foods worth knowing

- **Leafy greens, broadly.** Spinach, bok choy, chrysanthemum greens (茼蒿, tóng hāo), pea shoots, watercress, young mustard greens. The workhorse of the Wood-liver list and the place modern nutrition and the tradition shake hands: folate, fiber, and plant compounds on one side, "ascending green food for the Wood season" on the other. Cook them quickly — a 90-second blanch, a hot-pan toss with a dab of sesame oil.
- **Sprouts.** Mung, soybean, lentil, alfalfa — caught literally mid-growth, which is the point. Convenient and widely available.
- **Celery and cucumber.** The high-water greens that cool and move at once; celery especially is the traditional "calm the liver" vegetable in southern Chinese home cooking.
- **Broccoli and broccolini.** The Western-green stand-in that fits the category perfectly: dense, fresh, and easy to roast or steam.
- **Green herbs — cilantro, mint, dill, scallion, young ginger (生姜, shēng jiāng).** The "gentle pungency" the classics keep recommending; a handful at the end of a dish does more in this framework than any single superfood.
- **Sour green apple and unripe plum.** The green-sour end of the palette, used sparingly to gather and consolidate rather than to flood.
- **Goji berry (枸杞, gǒu qǐ) leaves and shoots.** Less common in the West, but the leaf is the green version of the famous red berry, and both are traditionally aimed at the liver and eyes.

## What the tradition tells you to ease off

The subtractions matter as much as the additions. Heavy, greasy, deep-fried food in spring fights the season. Large amounts of very sour or heavily pickled food over-tighten the liver's flow rather than support it — a squeeze of lemon is nothing, a daily jar of pickles is the excess the classics warn against. Alcohol is the universal spring caution. And aggressive "liver detox" protocols are a modern invention wearing a traditional label; the tradition's spring guidance is food, movement, and patience, not a three-day juice fast.

## How to build a green week

Three small levers. First, one green vegetable a day, cooked quickly rather than long, so it stays bright and upward-moving rather than heavy. Second, one fresh aromatic finish — scallion, chives, cilantro, a little grated ginger. Third, a weekly green cup: a mild green tea, or chrysanthemum (菊花, jú huā) if your head runs hot. Then watch your late-afternoon mood rather than anything you can measure — that is the honest scale for this kind of practice.

## Green food, seen in the wider map

Green is one corner of a five-color system. If you only ever eat green, you have covered Wood and left the other four elements thin — which is exactly the mistake a "salad every day" habit can drift into. The fix is not less green; it is a red, yellow, white, and black food somewhere in the same week, so the liver gets its color and the rest of the system gets theirs.

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I get noticeably more irritable once spring arrives | ☐ |
| Rich, fried, or heavy meals sit worse in April than December | ☐ |
| I carry tension in my shoulders, jaw, or the sides of my head | ☐ |
| My plate is overwhelmingly green and beige, little else | ☐ |
| My eyes feel dry or tired by late afternoon | ☐ |
| Getting outside and moving resets my mood faster than rest | ☐ |

Four or more checks suggests the Wood-spring pattern is genuinely yours, and a few weeks of the food shift above is a reasonable experiment. One or two is unremarkable — almost everyone borrows a little Wood.

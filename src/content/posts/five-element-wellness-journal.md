The notebook is on the shelf above the coat rack, three weeks filled and the other hundred pages blank. You bought it in September, when a page of prompts looked like a reasonable way to pay attention. Then a Tuesday went sideways, one evening got skipped, and the blank stretch started doing what blank stretches do.

Nothing was wrong with the questions. The problem was arithmetic. A journal that wants a paragraph about your mood, a gratitude list, three highlights, and a note on the day's eating is asking for four hundred words, and four hundred words at 10:40 at night is a second job.

There is a smaller version, and the tradition underneath it is far less wordy than the modern shelf suggests. What follows is a way to keep a record of a body and a season. It is not medical advice, not an assessment, and it does not diagnose anything.

## Ten lines, six prompts

Rule the page once and reuse it. Date and wake time get the first line. Five prompts take the other nine.

| Prompt | Lines |
| --- | --- |
| Hour you woke | 1 |
| One word for how the body felt | 1 |
| Where you felt it | 1 |
| First thing you ate, and whether it was warm | 1 |
| One thing you put down today | 3 |
| One sentence to tomorrow-you | 3 |

A college-ruled page with one-inch margins holds about thirty-two lines of writing, which makes ten lines three days. A 120-page notebook, then, runs most of a year. That number is worth doing before you buy anything, because the reason journals close in week two is usually that the format assumed a person with an empty evening.

Two prompts get three lines and four get one. The four single-line prompts cannot be answered with a paragraph, which is the entire design. Ask "how do you feel" and you get a paragraph of hedging. Ask for one word and you get the word you would not have written otherwise.

Two findings bracket the idea, and they point in the same direction. Expressive writing, the fifteen-to-twenty-minute kind studied since the 1980s, is for upheaval: write about the thing that is bothering you on three or four consecutive days, then stop. A log is a different instrument. In a 2008 Kaiser Permanente study of roughly 1,700 adults, the people who kept a food record six or more days a week lost about twice as much over six months as the people who kept none. Neither result claims a notebook improves your health. Both suggest the recording is the active ingredient, and both reward the version you will actually fill in.

## The margin is the part you read

The one-word prompt goes in the left margin, written larger than everything else. Not in the body of the entry.

That single decision is what makes the notebook worth keeping. At the end of a week you will not re-read seven paragraphs. You will read seven words, and only if they sit where your eye falls.

Five words cover most of what shows up.

| Word | Where it usually lands | Element |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tight, braced | jaw, shoulders, the sigh you keep doing | Wood |
| hot, wired | face, chest, the 3 a.m. wake-up | Fire |
| heavy, foggy | stomach, limbs, an hour after eating | Earth |
| dry, raw | throat, nose, skin, a breath that stops short | Metal |
| cold, hollow | lower back, knees, the reserve | Water |

The third prompt asks where, not why, and the old books explain the order. The Plain Questions files each emotion with an organ, grief with the lungs and anger with the liver (忧伤肺, 怒伤肝), in the chapter on yin and yang correspondences (《素问·阴阳应象大论》). The pairing is not a claim that feelings damage tissue. It is an argument that a feeling has a location, and the location is the part you can observe. "Where" is answerable at 10:40 at night. "Why" is not.

## Seven minutes on Sunday

Tally the margin, not the entries. Count the words.

A word that appears on four or more days out of seven is the week's signature, and it is the only thing on the page worth acting on. A word that appears once is either noise or the surprise, and the surprise is worth a sentence in the three-line prompt before you go to bed.

Here is where the reading pass usually goes wrong. The instinct is to feed whichever element has the most words. Read it the other way. A leader that shows up all week is a phase doing its job under load, and the element that has gone quiet is the one running on empty. The same rule governs the [full self-care routine](/blog/five-element-self-care-routine/): you rarely correct your dominant phase, you feed what is depleted.

Two more things the tally can tell you, both cheap. A word that repeats for three consecutive weeks has stopped being a week and become a standing condition, which is a conversation for a clinician rather than a page. And a word that never appears is usually the prompt you are skipping.

## What the season already asked for

The chapter on the four seasons in the Plain Questions (《素问·四气调神大论》) is, oddly for a medical text, a set of instructions about clock time. Autumn asks you to retire early and rise early, getting up with the roosters (早卧早起，与鸡俱兴). Winter asks you to retire early and rise late, and to wait for the light before going out (早卧晚起，必待日光). Spring and summer flip the morning: late to bed, early up.

That gives the first column a second job. Once a season, compare your average wake time against what the season asked for. Through October the instruction is still an early rise, and through most of November it is still an early rise, and then at the turn it becomes a late one. The winter line is the only place in the whole system where a later alarm is the traditional answer rather than a failure, and it lasts about six weeks.

Most people find the opposite drift. The wake time creeps later across the autumn while the light is disappearing at five, and the entry that shows it is the one nobody would have thought to write.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I have bought a journal and stopped inside a month | ☐ |
| My last attempt asked for paragraphs | ☐ |
| I write at night, when I have the least patience | ☐ |
| I could name a body sensation but not its cause | ☐ |
| I have never compared two weeks of anything | ☐ |
| I would fill in ten lines if that were the whole ask | ☐ |

Three or more and the format is the fix, not your discipline. If only the first one is true, the notebook on the shelf is probably still usable; rule the page tonight and start on tomorrow rather than on the first of the month.

## Tonight, ten lines

Take the notebook you already own and rule the next page into six boxes with a margin down the left side. Write tomorrow's date and leave the hour blank until morning. The first week is a baseline, so do not change anything you eat or do, and do not read the margin until Sunday.

One caveat, and it is the same one that belongs on every page in this section. A word that keeps surfacing is a pattern in your attention, not a finding about your body. If the same one has been there for a month, or it arrives with weight loss, a fever, or a symptom that wakes you, the notebook has done its job by pointing at it, and the next step is a person with a stethoscope.

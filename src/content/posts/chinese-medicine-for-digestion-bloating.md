Almost everyone who walks into a Chinese medicine practice mentions their stomach. Bloating is that common, and it's also why this system is useful here: instead of one remedy for "bloating," it asks what kind, and treats the answer as the interesting part. Four patterns account for most ordinary cases, and they ask for noticeably different changes.

This is traditional theory and everyday food-and-habit guidance, not medical advice. Bloating that is new, severe, or persistent deserves a clinician — and certain signs skip this article entirely and go straight to a doctor: blood in the stool, unintentional weight loss, fever, difficulty swallowing, pain that wakes you at night, or anything that's been getting steadily worse for weeks. Everything below is about the ordinary, meal-shaped discomfort that isn't those things.

## The question this system asks first

Conventional advice tends to start with *what food caused it*. Chinese medicine starts with *when and how it feels*, because timing carries the diagnosis.

Ask yourself three things for a week: Does bloating arrive within thirty minutes of eating, or does it build through the afternoon regardless of what you ate? Is it worse during stressful weeks than during relaxed ones? Does pressure, heat, or passing gas relieve it? Those answers sort four patterns apart faster than any food-elimination spreadsheet.

## Pattern one: food stagnation (食滞, shí zhì)

The name is literal: the meal is sitting there. It shows up soon after eating, increases with volume, and typically follows the rich ones — a big dinner, too much wine, a plate that deserved to be stopped halfway. Burps taste sour, passing stool or gas brings obvious relief, and the appetite for the *next* meal disappears.

What it asks for is smaller and less, not different. The traditional moves here are bitter and pungent — the two flavors that move rather than build. A cup of roasted dandelion or barley tea after a heavy meal, cooked rather than raw food for a day or two, and a fifteen-minute walk, which is the cheapest prokinetic anyone has suggested to you.

## Pattern two: damp with weak spleen (脾虚湿困, pí xū shī kùn)

This is the one that reads as "I'm just bloated all the time." It doesn't track a single meal; it swells through the day, usually worst in the afternoon, and comes with heaviness rather than cramping — limbs that feel wrapped, thinking that slow down, stool that trends loose.

In this reading the center isn't transforming fluids efficiently, and what accumulates is called damp (湿, shī). What it asks for is warmth and simplicity for a sustained period: fewer cold and raw items, less dairy if you notice it matters, more cooked vegetables and grains, and several weeks rather than several days. ## Pattern three: Liver overacting on Earth (肝木克土, gān mù kè tǔ)

Worth knowing even if you never use the formula: stress doesn't create a stomach problem, it changes how the stomach behaves. This is the bloating whose intensity tracks your calendar more than your menu. Tightness high up, under the ribs; frequent sighing; alternating with loose stool rather than consistently one way.

Notice what doesn't help — another elimination diet or another probiotic. What tends to help is the thing you did last time you were relaxed: eating seated, before a screen-free ten minutes; unhurried exhale-heavy breathing *before* the meal, not after; and a real walk outside. If this paragraph felt aimed at you, [How to Move Stagnant Liver Qi with Food](/blog/how-to-move-stagnant-liver-qi-food/) and [Worry and Digestion in Chinese Medicine](/blog/worry-and-digestion-tcm/) both go considerably deeper.

## Pattern four: cold in the middle (寒, hán)

Cramping rather than swelling, urgent relief from a hot-water bottle or a hot drink, and a clear pattern of mornings being worse after iced drinks, big salads, smoothies, or anything cold eaten late. This isn't a food allergy; in this frame it's a temperature problem, and it's the fastest of the four to improve.

What it asks for is warmth applied consistently: ginger and other warming cooking (姜黄, jiāng huáng and fresh ginger both belong here), cooked rather than raw food while you're symptomatic, a warm breakfast rather than chilled yogurt, and room-temperature water instead of iced. Two weeks of this usually tells you whether you've found your pattern.

## A week of changes that move most of them

Whichever pattern applies, these four rarely hurt and often help.

**Make your evening meal smaller and earlier.** Everything in this system points here. The center gets to rest overnight, and the following morning improves.

**Chew more than feels necessary.** Twenty-ish times is the traditional figure, which sounds silly until you notice how much air goes down with unchewed food.

**Walk after the largest meal.** Every pattern above includes it. Ten minutes is genuinely sufficient.

**Drink between meals, not with them.** Holding this one line often knocks the loudest symptom down a notch for people who drink large volumes with food.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I'm most bloated within thirty minutes of finishing a meal | ☐ |
| The bloating builds through the afternoon no matter what I ate | ☐ |
| My stomach is noticeably worse during stressful weeks | ☐ |
| Hot drinks or a hot-water bottle clearly relieve it | ☐ |
| My evenings are my heaviest eating window | ☐ |

The pattern you match isn't a verdict — most people read as two at once. Start with whichever line you recognized most immediately; that's usually the one with the most room in it.

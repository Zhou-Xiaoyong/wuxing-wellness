Eight days out, the beverage aisle. The cart already holds a case of eleven-dollar Pinot Noir, a magnum with a turkey printed on the label, and a twelve-pack of nonalcoholic beer because your sister stopped drinking in March. Nobody in the store knows how much of it will actually get opened.

The drinks are the last thing decided and the first thing poured. That order is backwards, and it is the reason the toasts at an American Thanksgiving land around 3:40 in the afternoon, on a stomach holding crackers and one deviled egg.

What follows is the traditional reading of that half of the table, plus the arithmetic. It is not medical advice. If you take a medication whose label warns against alcohol, if you are pregnant, or if you are the one driving, the number of glasses is zero and nothing below changes that.

## The toast lands before the food

You stand up to give the toast before anyone has a plate. In most houses the sequence runs: crackers, cheese, the toast, carving, sit down. So the first drink of the day arrives on an empty stomach, and an empty stomach is the fastest route alcohol has into your blood.

Alcohol is absorbed mostly in the small intestine, and food in the stomach holds that door shut for a while. Fat and protein slow the emptying more than bread does, which is why the cheese board is doing quiet work before dinner. Peak blood alcohol after drinking with no food comes at roughly thirty minutes. With a real meal it is lower and it arrives an hour or more later.

The carbonation detail matters here. A champagne toast is the fastest option on the table because dissolved gas moves the whole process along, and a sparkling cocktail is not far behind. If you want the toast to count for something other than speed, pour it after people have food in front of them. One change of sequence, and it is the largest single effect available in this article.

## What the tradition files alcohol under

The Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) files wine (酒, jiǔ) as bitter, sweet and pungent, greatly hot, and toxic (苦甘辛，大热，有毒). Blunt, especially from a text that is mild about most foods. Toxic here is a classification rather than a scare: it means this is not something you build a daily routine on. The tradition's own position is that wine is medicine-sized, not food-sized.

The working term is damp-heat (湿热, shī rè). Wine is warming and wet at the same time, and that pairing is the one Earth handles worst, Earth being the spleen and the business of digestion. Set that against the menu and the argument makes itself. A Thanksgiving plate is the sweetest, fattest, softest meal most American kitchens cook all year. Wine does not sit beside that meal. It stacks on top of it.

Which is the old case for two glasses and a long table, and against the third refill. Four hundred years on, it does not ask you to believe anything.

## The bottle math for twelve

A US standard drink is 12 ounces of 5% beer, 5 ounces of 12% wine, or 1.5 ounces of 40% spirits. A 750 ml bottle holds 25.4 ounces, which is five 5-ounce glasses, not the six or seven people assume when they set a bottle out.

Twelve adults at two drinks each is twenty-four drinks, about five bottles. Subtract the two guests who will have one and the one who is not drinking, and you are at three bottles plus a twelve-pack of something without alcohol.

The mistake is buying eight bottles because it is a holiday. What actually happens is that three get opened, two go flat on the counter by Friday noon, and the rest sit in the closet until next November. Four bottles of wine, one twelve-pack of NA beer, and a case of sparkling water is a table that runs out of nothing and wastes nothing.

Two footnotes that change the count. A hard seltzer or canned cocktail at 5% is one drink, same as a beer. And the bourbon poured at home is usually closer to two ounces than one and a half, so a single glass is really one and a third drinks. Meanwhile a red recorked and refrigerated on Thursday is still fine on Sunday, which is the argument for opening the better bottle rather than saving it.

## The cup after the pie

The last decision of the meal is what goes with dessert, and coffee at eight at night is a poor answer if you want to sleep. The traditional one is a warm infusion of hawthorn and aged tangerine peel.

Hawthorn (山楂, shān zhā) is the classic for food stagnation (食积, shí jī), and the Compendium names it specifically for meat and fat. Dried tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí) is the partner that moves things along and dries the damp. Together they taste tart with a bitter orange edge, which is precisely the note a plate of sugar and butter is missing.

Six to eight dried hawthorn slices and one two-inch strip of peel, four cups of water, simmered eight minutes then steeped five more. Serve it in small cups, four ounces each, honey on the side for anyone who wants it. It is caffeine-free, so it does not buy you a two a.m. wake-up, and it costs less than the port you were going to open anyway.

A caveat, and a real one: hawthorn is traditionally avoided in pregnancy, and it can interact with heart and blood pressure medication. Ask a pharmacist before you set it in front of a guest on digoxin.

If you want the same idea as a breakfast rather than a drink, [ginger and millet congee](/recipes/congee-settled-stomach/) is the morning version of this whole argument.

## Three things the host controls

Put the water pitcher on the table. People drink what is within reach and skip what sits on the counter behind them.

Do not top off glasses. A refill nobody asked for is a drink nobody counted, and the guest holding it has lost track too.

Have one option that is not sweet. Sparkling water with a strip of orange peel and a rosemary sprig reads as a drink rather than a consolation, and the guest who is not drinking will notice that you thought about it.

The last one is not a host move at all. If you have had two glasses and the drive home is forty minutes, coffee at nine does not change the arithmetic. Time does. Roughly an hour per standard drink is the rate the liver works at, and no amount of bread moves it. Body weight, food and medication all shift that figure a little. The only number that holds for certain is zero, and it holds on the nights you are the driver.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I have never counted how many glasses a 750 ml bottle actually pours | ☐ |
| The first toast at my table happens before anyone has a plate | ☐ |
| I buy the wine by feel rather than by headcount | ☐ |
| There is nothing on my table to drink that isn't sweet, besides water | ☐ |
| The water pitcher lives on the counter, not the table | ☐ |
| I have had coffee at nine to sober up before driving | ☐ |

Three or more and the drinks half of the table is running on habit rather than on a decision. The rows that matter most are the second and the last: one is a sequencing change you can make in ten seconds, and the other is the only one on this list with a hard limit.

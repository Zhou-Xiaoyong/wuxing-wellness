The first five elements chart I ever printed came out of a library printer at eight-point type. Every correspondence on it was correct. None of it was legible past arm's length, which is a problem for a page whose only job is being glanced at from the stove.

That's the usual failure: a reference table built for a screen, sent to a printer, read once from arm's length. Paper asks a different question. What do you need to see from six feet away, with your hands wet?

This is a traditional framework for noticing patterns, and what follows is a chart, not medical advice. It won't tell you what's wrong with you. It's closer to a calendar than a diagnosis: something to glance at while you decide what to cook on a Tuesday in October.

## Why most charts fail on paper

A Letter sheet with half-inch margins leaves 7.5 inches. Split that five ways and each element column gets 1.5 inches, roughly 20 characters at nine-point Helvetica. "Small intestine" plus its two Chinese characters does not fit in 20 characters unless you shrink the type. Shrink the type and you've built something that only works held in the hand.

The classical correspondences are also longer than people expect. In the *Inner Canon* (《黄帝内经》), the *Greater Discussion of Yin and Yang* (《素问·阴阳应象大论》) lines the five phases up as a sequence of generation — east gives rise to wind, wind gives rise to Wood, and so on through flavor, color, organ, and sound. That's an argument laid out in rank after rank. Beautiful as prose. Impossible as a wall chart.

So the printable below is not a smaller version of the full grid. It's a different object.

## The printable chart

Five rows, six columns, sized to survive a laser printer and a steam-filled kitchen.

| | Season | Organ | Flavor & color | Reach for | What shows up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Wood** (木) | Spring | Liver | Sour (酸), green | Lemons, vinegar, young greens, dandelion | Tight shoulders, sighing, waking near 2 a.m. |
| **Fire** (火) | Summer | Heart | Bitter (苦), red | Bitter greens, watermelon, tomato, red beans | Flushed face, restless sleep, bitter taste |
| **Earth** (土) | Late summer | Spleen | Sweet (甘), yellow | Winter squash, millet, yam, sweet potato | Heavy limbs, loose stools, no morning appetite |
| **Metal** (金) | Autumn | Lungs | Pungent (辛), white | Pears, daikon, garlic, almonds | Dry throat, dry skin, a cough that starts at night |
| **Water** (水) | Winter | Kidneys | Salty (咸), black | Black beans, walnuts, seaweed, bone broth | Cold low back, cold feet, waking to urinate |

Read a row left to right and it becomes one sentence: this season, this organ, this flavor, these foods.

The last column is the one people turn into a diagnosis. It isn't one. "Cold low back" shows up in a hundred ordinary situations, from a bad office chair to the weather. Treat the column as a question you can ask yourself, not an answer written down.

## Printer settings that actually matter

Four settings decide whether this prints as a chart or as a fax.

**Paper: Letter, portrait, 100%.** Not "Fit to page." Browsers default to fitting content inside the printable area, which quietly rescales a page by a few percent, and a few percent is the difference between nine-point and eight-point. Choose Actual size or 100% and let the margins fall where they fall.

**Margins: 0.25 inch, or None.** Half-inch margins are for documents with headers. This page doesn't have any.

**Headers and footers: off.** Every browser adds the page URL and a date across the bottom by default. A printed URL along the edge of a chart makes it look like a printout rather than a reference. Uncheck the box.

**Background graphics: on.** Turn this off and any filled header cells print white, and the rules can go pale. If you're printing on a toner-saver setting, expect the hairline rules between rows to disappear entirely. Thicker rules are the fix, not darker text.

One test print in grayscale is worth the sheet of paper. Taped inside a kitchen cabinet, a color-coded chart fades to three shades of gray, and the element names are what you'll actually be reading.

## What I cut to fit one page

The full grid runs to eleven correspondence rows. This sheet carries four, folded into two columns, plus two the classical chart never had. The removals were deliberate.

The emotion row went first. Anger, joy, overthinking, grief, fear — it's the most seductive row on any five elements chart and the one most often misread as a personality verdict. Two of the five emotions are also not emotions in English at all, which is a long explanation that a fridge door cannot hold.

The organ clock went next. The two-hour peak times mean nothing without a paragraph about what "peak" is supposed to describe, and printed alone at the bottom of a chart they read as a schedule you're already late for.

Direction went because east, south, center, west, and north are cosmology, not cooking. On a wall it just looks like a compass someone left there.

The yang organ went for space. Gallbladder, small intestine, stomach, large intestine, bladder: five entries that send you to a book once and never come up during dinner prep.

Sense organ, tissue, and climate went with them: eyes, tendons, wind. Entries you look up once.

What stayed is the part that changes a decision at the store or the stove. Season because you can feel it. Organ because it's the vocabulary the tradition uses. Flavor and color because they translate into a shopping list. Foods because a chart that doesn't tell you what to buy is trivia.

## Four weeks with a pencil

A printed chart has one advantage a webpage doesn't: you can write on it.

Week one, circle the column you land in most days. Not the one you'd like to be. Most people pick the flattering element in about four seconds and the accurate one in about four weeks.

Week two, tick the flavor you actually ate each day. Almost everyone finds two flavors dominating and one entirely absent. The absent one is usually the flavor the current season is asking for.

Week three, cook one food from the "reach for" cell of your circled column three times. Three is enough to notice whether it changes anything.

Week four, write the date next to any body signal that shows up in the last column. If a single signal repeats on four dates in one month, that's a pattern worth mentioning to a doctor rather than a chart.

A month of pencil marks is a diary, and a diary is not a diagnosis. The moment four ticks become a finding, the chart stops helping.

## The honest limit

Paper doesn't make a framework truer, only more present. If the chart sits on the fridge for a month and you never once look at it, the framework isn't wrong, it's just not how you think. Some people need the sheet. Some need a pot of soup and a note about ginger.

Print one page, keep a pencil nearby, and give it four weeks. If a signal repeats for longer than that, or it hurts, or it frightens you, put the pencil down and see a clinician. The chart's job ends where that conversation starts.

The bruise is on the back of your thigh, the size of a quarter, and you cannot account for it. You did not walk into a table. It is not sore. It has been there four days and it has gone from purple to a greenish yellow, which is the body doing its ordinary work.

Most pages about the spleen, including several here, tell you what to eat. Warm, cooked, naturally sweet. Millet, yam, squash, congee. All of that serves one job, transformation, the one the tradition spends the most words on.

There is a second job, and it is the one most articles skip. In this system the spleen also keeps blood where it belongs. Not making it. Not moving it. Holding it in.

What follows is a traditional reading of that job and a short way to work with it. It is not medical advice. Bruising and bleeding have a long list of causes, and several of them need a blood test before they need a grocery list.

## Three owners of blood, and the one that gets skipped

Classical physiology hands blood to three systems, each with a different verb.

The heart drives it. The liver stores it, which is why the [liver page](/blog/how-to-strengthen-liver-naturally-chinese-medicine/) here is about filling an empty tank. The spleen keeps it inside the pipes.

The Classic of Difficulties (《难经·四十二难》) gives the spleen an anatomical description and, partway through it, four characters that became a doctrine: it governs the wrapping of blood (主裹血). Wrap (裹, guǒ) is a cloth verb. It is what you do to something soft that would otherwise spread.

So the spleen is not a pump or a filter in this picture. It is the wall. Later physicians said so plainly. Tang Rongchuan, writing on bleeding disorders in 1884 (《血证论》), put it in nine characters: blood moves up and down entirely because of the spleen (血之运行上下，全赖乎脾).

## Where a failed wrap is visible

You cannot feel a spleen holding blood. You can see the five places where the container reports.

Gums that bleed when you brush, with no dental cause. Nosebleeds without a dry week behind them. Blood where it does not belong, in stool or urine. Skin marks. And for anyone who menstruates, the period, the largest and most measurable of the five.

The marks have names worth knowing, because they mean different things. Pinpoint dots under 2 mm that do not fade when pressed are petechiae. Patches from 2 mm to a centimeter are purpura. Anything larger is an ecchymosis, the clinical word for a bruise.

A bruise also keeps a readable clock. Hemoglobin goes red-blue in the first day or two, then green as it breaks into biliverdin, then yellow as bilirubin, then a brown that is hemosiderin. The order is dependable. The days attached to each stage are not, which is why forensic texts argue about them and clinicians do not.

Most easy bruising is not a spleen pattern. In older adults the commonest kind sits on the back of the forearms, painless, from two decades of sun thinning the collagen that cushions small vessels. The vessel is fine. The padding under it is not. Sunscreen and sleeves do more for that than any food.

## Ten days is the unit, and it is the fastest one here

The kidney piece in this series said ninety days, because hair and bone run on seasons. This one runs on ten.

A platelet circulates for eight to ten days before it is retired. The clotting factors built from vitamin K turn over faster still: factor VII has a half-life near six hours, prothrombin about two and a half days. The vessel lining renews in days.

That gives the whole subject a testable horizon. A week of the right food can change the wall you have next week. A week of a tonic cannot change a hair cycle, which is why so many people judge the wrong thing in the wrong month.

It also explains a piece of paperwork you may have signed. Ibuprofen and aspirin block a platelet enzyme irreversibly, and a platelet cannot make a new one, so the effect lasts the cell's whole life. That is why elective surgery asks you to stop a week ahead rather than a day.

## What actually supports the wall

Four things, all food, none of them exotic.

**Greens, with fat in the same pan.** Vitamin K1 is what the liver needs to build factors II, VII, IX and X. The adequate intake is 90 micrograms a day for women, 120 for men. A cup of cooked kale carries over 1,000, so the target is easy to clear; the trap is that K1 is fat-soluble, and a dry salad absorbs a fraction of what oil lets you take. Saute them.

**Vitamin C, daily.** Collagen is what the vessel wall is built from, and the enzyme that cross-links it needs ascorbate. Scurvy is the experiment that proved it: bleeding gums and pinpoint hemorrhages around hair follicles, which is a container failing in public. Half a red bell pepper covers a day's needs several times over.

**Enough protein.** The wall is protein and the factors are protein. Dull, and it matters more than any single herb.

**Fewer painkillers than you think.** If you take ibuprofen most days for a back that hurts, your platelets are partly offline most of the time. That is a real trade, worth saying out loud.

Iron belongs here with a caveat. A period that soaks protection in an hour is not a pattern to support with food; it is over 80 milliliters a month and the leading cause of iron deficiency before menopause. Molasses has iron. It does not replace a ferritin number, and 30 ng/mL is where the fatigue literature starts.

## The formula named for this job

The classical answer to a failed wrap has a name that reads like an instruction. Return to the Spleen (归脾汤, guī pí tāng) was recorded by Yan Yonghe in 1253 (《济生方》) and still sits on pharmacy shelves in China.

Read the ingredient list and it looks like a pantry: astragalus, longan flesh, jujube, ginger, licorice. Read what it is prescribed for and the doctrine turns concrete. It is given for this exact pattern, blood that will not stay in, and also for palpitations and broken sleep. One formula, two complaints, because the tradition treats them as one event. Blood that leaves is blood the heart no longer has, and the spirit rides on blood (血气者，人之神, 《素问·八正神明论》).

## What earns a doctor instead of a pot

Marks that arrive in clusters and do not blanch. Bruising on the back, palms or soles, where you cannot knock yourself. Gums that bleed despite good hygiene. A period needing a pad an hour for more than two hours. Any bleeding on a blood thinner. Bruising with fever, night sweats, or weight you did not intend to lose.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I find bruises I cannot account for more than once a month | ☐ |
| My gums bleed when I brush and my dentist has found no cause | ☐ |
| A small cut oozes longer than it used to | ☐ |
| I take ibuprofen or aspirin most days of the week | ☐ |
| My period soaks through protection in an hour on at least one day | ☐ |
| I bruise on the backs of my forearms, and I spent years in the sun | ☐ |

Four or more, and the pattern deserves a blood count before a grocery list. Two or fewer, and the bruise on your thigh is doing what bruises do.

You can read the phrase "move liver qi" (疏肝, shū gān) as a metaphor, or you can read it as a kitchen instruction. In the Chinese wellness tradition it is both. The liver, paired with the Wood phase (木, mù), is the system that keeps energy moving in smooth, upward strokes; when that movement stalls, the tradition calls the result stagnation, and it reaches for food first, not supplements. This is a look at the specific foods that, in that framework, help stuck energy find a way forward — and how to actually put them on the plate so they do the job.

A note before the recipes: this is a traditional self-care perspective, not medical advice, and not a substitute for a clinician — especially if you have a diagnosed liver condition, persistent pain, or you are taking medication. Treat the ideas below as cultural practice with a modern feel: a ritual, not a treatment.

## Why food is the first move

The logic is physical. Wood is the phase of spring, of upward growth, green, and the flavor Sour (酸, suān). Its natural state is movement, and the dietary approach follows directly: you eat in a way that supports flow and ease off the things that, in this model, clog or inflame it. The point is not to "cleanse" the liver in the marketing sense. It is to stop adding friction and give pooled energy somewhere to go.

That makes food a channel, not a prescription. A sour-and-green plate will not dissolve a hard deadline, but it can stop your meals from making the tightness worse — and over a few weeks of repetition, it gives the liver something to work with.

## The movers: herbs and ingredients that shift Wood

A handful of traditional allies do the heavy lifting, and they are the ones Wood naturally likes.

- **Rose buds (玫瑰花, méi guī huā).** The classic flower for gently moving stuck liver energy. A few buds steeped in hot water is the most direct version of the whole idea — floral, slightly moving, easy to keep on the counter.
- **Aged citrus peel (陈皮, chén pí).** Dried tangerine or orange peel, the older the better, used to loosen and guide stuck energy downward through digestion. A small piece goes into teas and broths.
- **Chrysanthemum flowers (菊花, jú huā).** Used for the heat that builds behind stagnation — the flushed, irritable, wired end of the pattern. Cool and grassy, it pairs with rose when the knot has a hot edge.
- **Sour is the signal flavor.** Lemon, a little rice or apple-cider vinegar, plums, and sour cherries all carry the Sour (酸) note the classics say gathers and unbinds at once. Warm lemon water in the morning is the flavor-level version of the entire approach.
- **Green and bitter leaves.** Leafy greens, sprouts, celery, and the spring bitters (dandelion greens, endive, radicchio) are the visual stand-ins for Wood — read in this system as gently moving and clearing.
- **Mild nourishers.** Goji berries (枸杞, gǒu qǐ) and mung beans (绿豆, lǜ dòu) support the liver without adding heat, which is why they show up in so many spring routines.

## What to cook, and how

Preparation matters here as much as the ingredient, because the goal is movement, not heaviness.

- **Keep greens lightly cooked or raw-tender.** Long, soggy boiling is read as weighting the very system you want to lift. A quick steam, a stir-fry over high heat, or a raw salad keeps the moving quality intact.
- **Lead with a sharp dressing.** A vinegar-and-citrus vinaigrette over bitter greens is, in this framework, a liver-soothing meal in a bowl.
- **Ease off the heavies.** Fried food, late greasy dinners, and too much coffee are read as heating or clogging the channel Wood needs. You do not ban them; you lower the dose and move them earlier in the day.
- **A warm floral tea in the evening.** Rose or chrysanthemum, screens down, lights low — the same idea in a cup, timed to the liver's nighttime housekeeping window.

## A three-day "keep it moving" sequence

If the ingredient list feels abstract, here is the compressed version the tradition would actually endorse — repeat it as a loop rather than treat it as a one-off reset.

- **Day 1 — Sour start.** Warm lemon water at waking, a vinegar-dressed green salad at lunch, rosebud tea in the evening.
- **Day 2 — Green load.** Steamed leafy greens with dinner, a citrus-peel-and-goji tea in the afternoon, a short walk after eating.
- **Day 3 — Bitter clear.** A handful of dandelion or endive in the salad, chrysanthemum tea if the day ran hot, and lights down by 11 p.m.

The repetition is the method. Qi moves because you keep giving it channels, not because you ate one heroic salad.

## When food alone isn't enough

Food is one door into the same room. The stagnation that a sour-and-green plate helps also answers to movement (a daily walk), expression (the sentence you keep rescheduling), and sleep (the liver's 1–3 a.m. shift). The diet makes the other two easier; it does not replace them. Used together, they are the traditional answer to a pattern most modern life quietly manufactures.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I feel a band of tightness under my ribs or along my sides | ☐ |
| My digestion worsens on high-stress or high-friction days | ☐ |
| A little sour or green food noticeably lifts my mood | ☐ |
| Greasy, fried, or late heavy meals reliably make me feel worse | ☐ |
| I sigh audibly without noticing I'm doing it | ☐ |
| Alcohol or too much coffee leaves me tight and irritable the next day | ☐ |

Two or more checks means the moving-food approach is worth a month of your time. Zero checks means your Wood is already flowing — guard your sleep anyway.

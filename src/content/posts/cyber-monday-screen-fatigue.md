It is 11:40 on Saturday night, the phone is six inches from your face at the lowest brightness, and you are reading the fourth review of a sous-vide circulator you had no interest in on Thursday. Your eyes feel like they have sand in them. You could not name what is in the cart without opening it.

The sand is not the furnace. It is the looking. Blink rate falls by half or more when you read on a screen, the tear film breaks between those missing blinks, and the Inner Canon has a four-character entry for the whole transaction: prolonged looking injures the blood (久视伤血, jiǔ shì shāng xuè).

The clause sits in a short list in the chapter on the five qi of clarity (《素问·宣明五气篇》), five kinds of overuse and what each one draws down. Looking takes from the blood. Lying down takes from the qi. Sitting, standing, walking, one injury apiece. It treats staring as a load rather than a mood.

None of this is medical advice. Eyes that are gritty every day, or that water in a warm room in July, or that blur and clear and blur again through an afternoon, belong in an exam room rather than in a shorter shopping list.

## What the tradition means by blood here

Blood (血, xuè) in this system is not the number on a lab report. It is the substance that keeps tissue fed and moist, and the eyes are its most visible customer. The liver opens into the eyes (肝开窍于目), which is why the liver pages here always end up talking about vision, and why the tea at the end of this piece has goji in it.

Read that way, the four characters describe a supply line rather than a mood. Hold the eyes on close work for hours and you draw on something rebuilt during sleep and food, not during the next hour of the same work.

The evidence is a schedule rather than a scan. Eyes scratchy at three in the afternoon and clear at eight the next morning are not damaged. They were used.

## What three days of comparing costs

Blinking. At rest, most people blink fifteen to twenty times a minute. Reading on a screen drops that to five or seven, because a blink interrupts the sentence and the brain quietly cancels it. The tear film is thinner than a sheet of paper and breaks in about ten seconds when nothing sweeps over it. Fewer blinks, and the surface spends the afternoon broken. That is the sand.

Focus. Looking at something fourteen inches away means the ciliary muscle contracts to thicken the lens, and it stays contracted as long as the thing stays close. Three hours of tabs at that distance is three hours of a small muscle holding a pose built for seconds. The ache lands behind the eyes and just above the eyebrows. It usually gets blamed on the light coming off the screen. It is the muscle.

This is why the 20-20-20 rule exists, the one eye doctors hand out: every twenty minutes, look at something twenty feet away for twenty seconds. It is not rest. It releases one muscle and lets the tear film rebuild, which is the smallest thing that interrupts the pose.

Two things it does not need are a dark room and a pair of amber glasses. Trials of blue-light-blocking lenses have mostly failed to show a meaningful effect on eye strain, and the sleep research points somewhere less convenient: a lit screen at eleven keeps you up because of what it asks your attention to do, and because of the hour it moves your bedtime to. A price comparison at midnight is arousing. That is the mechanism.

## The bill arrives Monday morning

Three nights of scrolling past midnight, with a fourth coming on Sunday, costs about two and a half hours of sleep across the week. Two and a half hours will not make anyone feel sleepy. It is enough to feel flat: slower on the first email, unable to start the thing you meant to start.

That part follows you past the sale. The deals close at midnight Monday; the schedule you built over the weekend does not.

## Tonight, in this order

**9:00 p.m. Write three things down, then open the laptop.** What you actually needed, before Thursday. Three items, no adjectives. Buy those or don't. Everything else waits until Tuesday, when the wanting has usually drained out of it. The list is not a budget. It keeps a decision from being made by a page built to stop you from making one.

**Eat something warm, sitting down.** Nine o'clock on a deal weekend is usually a granola bar eaten standing at the counter, or nothing, or the third coffee. Warm cooked food at a table is the least exciting advice on this page and the one that changes the night. A pot of [ginger and millet congee](/recipes/congee-settled-stomach/) takes forty-five minutes and asks nothing of you after the first five. If it is already nine-thirty, a bowl still beats a bar at eleven.

**Ten minutes at distance. Outside if you can.** Not darkness. Distance. Stand on the porch and look at the farthest thing you can see, two minutes at a stretch. If the far thing is a tree, better, because the eye drifts across a tree instead of locking onto it. Three minutes of cold does more for the eleven o'clock fog than another cup of coffee.

**Bed at your usual hour, phone in another room.** This is the reset, and it is the step that gets skipped, because the cart is still open and Monday is still two days away. The tradition's account of why sleep repairs the eyes belongs to another page: [blood returns to the liver when you lie down](/blog/how-to-strengthen-liver-naturally-chinese-medicine/). A cup of [goji and red date tea](/recipes/goji-red-date-tea/) at nine is a decent way to end an evening. At midnight it is just another thing you are doing instead of sleeping.

## If you already bought it

The cart went through and the confirmation emails are stacked. Don't reopen the listing to prove the purchase was right. It is the same muscle doing the same work, and it will find what it is looking for either way.

Let the boxes sit where they land. An unopened box on the hall floor for two days answers what no amount of comparison could: whether you wanted the thing, or the looking. If it is still sealed on Tuesday, the return window is the useful part of what you bought. Thirty days is the common number, several large retailers carry late-November purchases into January, and the exact date lives on the policy page, not in an article. Put it in your calendar the day the box arrives, once.

What the weekend did to a budget is a different page than this one. What it did to your eyes is not.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I have compared the same three products on four different sites this weekend | ☐ |
| My eyes are scratchy by mid-afternoon and clear the next morning | ☐ |
| I have not focused on anything farther than the kitchen wall since Thursday | ☐ |
| My bedtime has moved later every night since Wednesday | ☐ |
| I plan to check the deals once more before I go to bed | ☐ |
| I could not have said what I needed before I started looking | ☐ |

Four or more and the fix is one evening rather than a supplement: three items on paper, warm food at a table, ten minutes of looking at something far away, and bed at the hour you normally keep. Two or fewer, and this weekend cost you less than it cost most people.

One caveat. If the sand is there at eight in the morning too, or the blur does not clear when you look up and blink, this is not a screen-weekend problem and no amount of distance will answer it.

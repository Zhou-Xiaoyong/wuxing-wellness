Saturday morning in the first week of November, the market table holds both halves of the year at once. The last field tomatoes, still faintly warm in the crate, next to the first butternut squash, hard enough to knock on. You buy both. Nothing about that is a compromise, and it is closer to how this system actually works than any food chart you will find online.

A seasonal eating guide in the Chinese tradition is a sequence of dates before it is a list of ingredients. The year turns four times, on four days that barely move, and each turn is announced roughly two weeks before the weather in your yard agrees to it. Read that gap as a prohibition and the whole thing sounds absurd. No more tomatoes in August, when August is the only month worth eating them.

What follows is the calendar itself: four dates, the eighteen days before each, and what is worth changing in the gap. It is a framework for cooking, not medical advice, and it diagnoses nothing. Anything you manage with a clinician outranks a calendar.

## Four days that do not move

The Chinese year is lunisolar, so the new year drifts. The agricultural markers do not. Those are the 24 solar terms (二十四节气, èr shí sì jié qì), each marking fifteen degrees of the sun's apparent path, which pins the set to the Gregorian calendar within a day. The complete list appears in the Huainanzi (《淮南子》) in the second century BC.

Four of the twenty-four are corners, and they land within a day of the same dates every year.

| Turn | Date | What changes in the kitchen | What the market is still selling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beginning of Spring (立春, lì chūn) | Feb 4 | Heat time drops. Blanch instead of braise | Storage roots, citrus, greenhouse greens |
| Beginning of Summer (立夏, lì xià) | May 5 | The braises stop, light soups start | Asparagus, peas, strawberries |
| Beginning of Autumn (立秋, lì qiū) | Aug 7 | One moistening food joins the plate | Tomatoes, corn, peaches at peak |
| Beginning of Winter (立冬, lì dōng) | Nov 7 | The long pot returns, ninety minutes and up | Squash, cabbage, roots, apples |

The second column is the calendar. The fourth is the supermarket. They do not line up, and the eighteen days between them are the entire subject.

## Eighteen days, and whose they are

The window is not a modern coach's invention. In the *Plain Questions* (《素问·太阴阳明论》), the emperor asks why the spleen has no season of its own, and the answer is that the spleen is earth, governs the center, and acts for each of the four seasons for eighteen days (脾者土也，治中央，常以四时长四脏，各十八日寄治).

So the tail of every season belongs to digestion. That explains what transition food looks like across the tradition: warm, cooked, plain, faintly boring. Not because rice porridge is magic, but because the organ on duty has to process whatever you send down.

The four-seasons chapter (《素问·四气调神大论》) supplies the other half. Nourish yang in spring and summer, yin in autumn and winter (春夏养阳，秋冬养阴). Read that as a statement about resources rather than temperature: the half of the year that stores is where the half that spends gets fed.

## February: the turn with nothing to buy

Beginning of Spring arrives with the ground frozen across most of the country. The tradition's spring list is greens, sprouts, and something sour. The aisle has rutabaga and citrus from California.

The change the calendar asks for in February is a method, not a purchase. Same cabbage: ninety seconds in boiling water instead of ninety minutes in the oven. Sour at the table, cider vinegar or lemon, rather than in the pot where it dulls. What actually shifts first in the produce aisle is the price of greens, and that happens in March.

## May: cool is not cold

Beginning of Summer lands in the best six weeks of the vegetable year. The tradition's instruction is to cool the kitchen, which most readers take as a licence for ice.

Cooling (凉) is a property the tradition assigns to a food, not a temperature you serve it at; mung beans are cooling and they go into a warm soup. The real change at this turn is that the oven stops. The ice question has its own date, closer to July.

## August: the disagreement that matters

This is the one that makes people give up. It is ninety-five degrees, the corn is perfect, and the calendar says begin moistening.

Two things are true. The dew point falls before the temperature does across much of the eastern U.S., so late-August air is drying while it is still hot. The hot spell that follows the turn even has its own name, the autumn tiger (秋老虎, qiū lǎo hǔ), because everyone has noticed that the calendar moved and the thermometer did not.

That split explains why autumn advice contradicts itself. Warm dryness (温燥, wēn zào) runs from this turn to around September 7, when the air is dry and the sweat has not stopped. Cool dryness (凉燥, liáng zào) starts after early October, when the air is dry and cold, and the food turns warmer to match: the pear goes into a soup with ginger rather than a steamer with rock sugar. Half the pages you read are written for one pattern, half for the other.

The move here is addition, not subtraction. One moistening thing next to the tomato salad, not instead of it.

## November: the turn where the market agrees

By the first week of November the table finally matches the calendar. Squash, cabbage, roots, apples, citrus. Nothing has to be argued.

Which makes it the turn people overdo. The instinct is escalation: hot pot, lamb, chili, more salt. The correspondence says salt enters the kidney (咸入肾), and the caution in the same breath says excess salt taxes the organ it is meant to support. What changes here is time, not heat. A ninety-minute braise does something a chili flake cannot.

Cabbage is the season's honest symbol, and the reason is storage. Cool cellars hold vitamin C in cabbage far better than a warm pantry does, which is why sauerkraut is usually credited with keeping scurvy off Captain Cook's second voyage in 1772. The gift was never the vegetable. It was the shelf life.

## The calendar was written for one latitude

The 24 terms were codified in the Yellow River basin, roughly 34 to 40 degrees north. That is the band through Philadelphia, Columbus, and northern Colorado, which is why the dates feel right in Ohio and early in Georgia.

Keep the order, move the dates. If your last frost comes later than mid-April, push the spring turn back by the difference and let the other three follow. Do not confuse the calendar with the supply chain: a January supermarket will sell you asparagus flown in from Peru.

## This week

Beginning of Winter is a few days out. Choose one thing to add, one to retire, and write both on a note stuck to the fridge. Eighteen days is long enough for a habit and too short to rebuild a pantry. Then put a pot on for ninety minutes.

One caveat, and it is the same one that belongs on every page in this section. A calendar is a way to decide what is for dinner. It cannot tell you why your hands are cold in a warm room, or why a cough has lasted a month. Those are questions for a person with a stethoscope, and the calendar has no opinion about them.

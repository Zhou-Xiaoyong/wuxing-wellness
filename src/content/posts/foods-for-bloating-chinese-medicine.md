Search for bloating advice and you get a list of things to stop eating. Chinese medicine tends to work in the other direction: before it removes anything from your plate, it asks what would help the middle of the body do its job — and then tries to make that food good enough to eat several times a week. That's the whole strategy in a sentence, and it's why the food list below is mostly a shopping list rather than a blacklist.

The logic is worth stating plainly. In this system the spleen-stomach is the body's kitchen (脾, pí is the spleen, which here means the whole digestive-and-transport function, not the organ your doctor measures). It receives food, transforms it into qi (气, qì) and blood, and sends what isn't needed downward. When the kitchen runs slow — too cold, too tired, too overloaded — food and fluids linger instead of moving. That lingering is what you feel as distension, heaviness, and that low-grade fullness that tails you all afternoon. So the answer is warmth, cooking, and a small amount of aromatic movement.

One frame before we start: this is traditional food guidance, not medical advice. Bloating that is new, severe, or arriving with weight loss, blood in the stool, vomiting, fever, or pain that wakes you at night belongs with a clinician, not a pantry. Everything below is for the ordinary, meal-shaped discomfort that isn't those things.

## The principle: warm, cooked, and a little aromatic

Three adjectives govern this whole shelf.

**Warm** because a cold kitchen works slowly. This is why the tradition is so insistent about cooked food and room-temperature drinks when the middle feels sluggish, and so suspicious of iced drinks alongside a meal.

**Cooked** because cooking is pre-digestion. Steaming, simmering, and stewing do part of the work before the food reaches you, which is precisely what a tired spleen is asking for.

**Aromatic** because scent moves. Almost every herb and food this system uses for stuck-feeling bellies is fragrant — and fragrance is the quality that disperses rather than accumulates. If you remember nothing else, remember that the antidote to "sitting heavy" is "smells good and is served hot."

## The aromatics: foods that move what's stuck

These are the ones to keep on hand, because a pinch changes an entire pot.

**Aged tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí)** is the workhorse. It's dried citrus peel, aged until the harshness is gone, and it's the traditional answer to a meal that feels parked. Simmer a strip in water for ten minutes and drink it after a heavy dinner, or drop a piece into congee or broth while it cooks.

**Fresh ginger (生姜, shēng jiāng)** warms and moves at the same time. Three thin slices in hot water before a meal is the simplest version; you can also grate it into cooked vegetables or rice.

**Fennel seed (小茴香, xiǎo huí xiāng)** is the after-dinner one. Chew a small pinch, or steep half a teaspoon in hot water. If you've ever wondered why Indian restaurants set out fennel, this is the same instinct wearing different clothes.

**Cardamom (砂仁, shā rén)** is the strongest of the group and the traditional ally of a stomach that's full but not processing. It's a cooking spice more than a tea — a pod cracked into a stew or a rice dish.

**Perilla leaf (紫苏, zǐ sū)** is the fresh one, widely used in East Asian cooking and traditionally paired with rich or cold dishes, especially anything fishy.

You don't need all five. Pick two and use a pinch daily. Consistency matters far more than the size of the dose.

## The grains and roots that rebuild the center

Aromatics move what's stuck. This group makes sure there's less to get stuck in the first place, by giving the kitchen easy work.

**Millet (小米, xiǎo mǐ)** is the gentlest grain on the list and the traditional first food of a recovering appetite. Cook it into a loose porridge with plenty of water.

**White rice congee** — rice simmered to a soft slurry, roughly one part grain to eight parts water — is the single most prescribed preparation in Chinese food therapy. It's not exciting. That's the point; a boring food is a food that asks nothing.

**Yam (山药, shān yào)** — the long, pale, slightly sticky Asian yam, sold fresh or dried — is the classic spleen-strengthening root. Steam it and eat it plain, or fold it into congee.

**Pumpkin and sweet potato** do the same job in a sweeter key. Roast them, mash them, or simmer them into soup; both are naturally soft, warm, and easy.

**Adzuki bean with barley (红豆, hóng dòu and 薏米, yì yǐ rén)** is the traditional pair for a body that feels heavy and puffy rather than simply full. Simmer them together into a thin, barely-sweet water and drink the liquid.

Notice how few of these are raw. That's deliberate.

## The bitters and a small amount of sour

Bitter is the flavor this system associates with descending and drying — exactly what you want after a rich meal. **Roasted dandelion root** and **roasted barley tea** are the everyday versions; both taste like coffee's mild cousin and both are traditionally drunk after eating.

Sour is more specific. **Hawthorn (山楂, shān zhā)**, sold as dried slices or in a tea, is the traditional food for a belly overloaded with meat and fat, and it's the one place a strong sour note belongs. A splash of vinegar in a dressing does something similar on a smaller scale. Don't overdo sour if your middle already feels tight or acidic.

## What to take off the plate for two weeks

Not forever — two weeks, as an experiment, so you can feel the difference.

The short list: iced drinks with meals, large raw salads as a main course, more than a little dairy if you've noticed it matters to you, sugar-heavy snacks between meals, and the big late dinner. That last one is the highest-yield change on the entire page. A smaller, earlier evening meal gives the kitchen eight quiet hours, and most people notice the following morning before they notice anything else.

## A day that quietly does this for you

**Morning:** warm congee or porridge with a slice of ginger. Not chilled yogurt, not a smoothie, not coffee on an empty stomach.

**Midday:** the largest cooked meal of the day, eaten seated, finished at about eighty percent full. Then ten minutes of walking — the cheapest digestive aid anyone has ever suggested to you.

**Afternoon:** when the slump arrives, barley or dandelion tea instead of the second coffee. If you want something for the fullness itself, [Best Tea for Bloating](/blog/best-tea-for-bloating/) walks through the shelf herb by herb.

**Evening:** smaller, earlier, cooked. If you'd like this as a recipe rather than a rule, [Congee for a Settled Stomach](/recipes/congee-settled-stomach/) is the bowl version, and [Barley & Adzuki Water](/recipes/barley-adzuki-water/) covers the heavier, puffier feeling.

Two more things worth trying, because they cost nothing: chew about twice as long as feels necessary, and drink between meals rather than in the middle of them. Both reduce the amount of work the middle has to do.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| My worst bloating arrives within half an hour of eating | ☐ |
| I feel heavy and puffy rather than sharply full | ☐ |
| Iced drinks or big raw salads reliably make it worse | ☐ |
| My evening meal is my largest and my latest | ☐ |
| I drink large amounts of liquid with my food | ☐ |
| Warm, cooked, fragrant food noticeably helps | ☐ |

If you matched most of these, the food list above isn't a restriction — it's simply a better-default pantry. Start with the warm breakfast and the earlier dinner; those two changes carry most of the weight.

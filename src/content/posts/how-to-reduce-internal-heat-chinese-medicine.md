You know the feeling even if you have never had a name for it. Your face is warm when the room is not. You sleep badly and wake with a dry mouth. Your skin breaks out along the jaw, your eyes feel gritty by mid-afternoon, and the whole system seems a few degrees ahead of the weather. Every summer this gets called "too much heat," and by September the person has usually tried three cooling teas and one cold-plunge routine and is no better off.

Here is why. In Chinese medicine, internal heat (内热, nèi rè) is not a temperature reading. It is a *quality* — the same way dampness is a quality of heaviness and stagnation is a quality of stuckness. Heat means things are moving too fast and drying out while they go: too fast in the digestion, in the emotions, in the hours you keep. So the answer is rarely "eat cold things." Often that makes it worse.

To be clear about scope: this is a traditional framework and a lifestyle perspective, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat anything. A persistent fever, unexplained night sweats, a thyroid change, or skin that is suddenly inflamed belongs with a clinician. What follows is the ordinary, low-grade version most people are describing.

## Heat is a direction, not a number

The tradition sorts the signs of heat by what they do. Heat rises, so it shows up at the top: face, eyes, throat, mouth, head. Heat dries, so it shows up as thirst, dry lips, dry stool, a red tongue with little coating. Heat agitates, so it shows up as restlessness, insomnia, irritability. And heat reddens, so it shows up on the skin and the tongue.

Notice how different that is from "I feel hot." Heat here is a pattern of *signs that agree with each other*. One warm face at three in the afternoon is not a pattern. A warm face plus insomnia plus a dry mouth plus a redder tongue plus irritability, sustained over weeks, is — and it tells you which direction the system has drifted in.

## The fork in the road: excess heat and deficient heat

This is the part almost every English-language article skips, and it is the part that decides whether cooling food helps you or hurts you.

**Excess heat** is heat with something behind it. You are genuinely hot, your face is actually flushed, you want cold drinks and they satisfy you, your bowels are dry, you feel strong but irritated, and you feel *worse* in heat and better in cool air. This is what a week of fried food, alcohol, spicy meals and four late nights produces. It is the heat of surplus, and here cooling food — mung bean (绿豆, lǜ dòu), winter melon, cucumber, bitter greens, chrysanthemum (菊花, jú huā) — genuinely helps, because there is a real surplus to drain.

**Deficient heat** is heat with nothing behind it. The heat is real but the person is depleted. The flush sits on the cheekbones only. Palms, soles and chest feel hot, especially at night. You wake sweating, you are thirsty but drink only a little, your sleep is shallow, your tongue is red but thin rather than coated, and you feel *tired* rather than strong. This is the pattern that shows up after months of overwork, short nights, and dieting.

Now here is the trap: cooling food makes deficient heat worse. There is nothing to drain. Hit a depleted system with cold, raw, bitter food and you cool the digestion instead — the heat returns by evening, now with a bloated stomach and loose stool on top. This pattern needs *nourishment* that is slightly cooling: pear, lily bulb (百合, bǎi hé), white fungus, cooked apple, egg, congee. Warm food, cooling nature. That combination confuses anyone who thinks in terms of temperature, and it is the single most important distinction in this article.

## How to tell a real heat flush from a false one

There is a third thing, and it is the one that sends people down the wrong road for years: a face that is red and warm while the body underneath is cold.

If your hands and feet are cold, your lower back or belly feels cold, you are not actually thirsty, and yet your cheeks or upper chest flush in the evening, that is not heat. In this framework it is heat pushed upward — yang floating because it is not anchored below. Unsettled nights, an over-stimulated week, or a largely raw-food diet can produce it. The tell: cold drinks feel unpleasant and make the flush worse, and warmth on the lower body feels good.

The answer is the opposite of cooling — warm the bottom, quiet the top. Warm food, warm feet, an earlier night, something salty and grounding in small amounts, and a cup of warm water at the end of the day rather than a cold one. One more quick read helps: **does cooling help or exhaust you?** If a cold drink genuinely refreshes you, that is real heat; if it makes your stomach uneasy and your hands colder, it is not.

## The pantry, used correctly

For genuine excess heat, two things do most of the work. **Mung bean (绿豆, lǜ dòu)** is the tradition's standard cooling legume: simmer it into a thin broth, barely sweetened, and drink the liquid. Our [Mung Bean & Lotus Tea](/recipes/mung-bean-lotus-tea/) is the summer version. **Chrysanthemum (菊花, jú huā)** is the cooling flower for eyes and head, and it pairs well with a few goji berries (枸杞, gǒu qǐ) for the person who is hot *and* tired — one part cooling, one part nourishing.

**Winter melon, cucumber, celery, watermelon, and bitter greens** cover the food side. Bitter is the flavor this system assigns to drying and descending, which is exactly what excess heat needs; [Bitter Foods and the Heart](/blog/bitter-foods-heart-chinese-medicine/) explains why a little bitterness does more than a lot.

For deficient heat, swap the list: cooked pear, lily bulb, lotus seed (莲子, lián zǐ), black sesame, egg, and congee — warm preparation, cooling nature, and enough protein that you are actually rebuilding. And whatever cooling food you choose, cook it.

## What builds heat without you noticing

The tradition's list of what builds heat is short and will sound familiar. **Late nights**, because sleep is where yin is restored. **Alcohol and fried food**, the combination the texts warn about by name. **Hot spices with nothing to balance them** — warming is useful, but a week of it is a heat pattern. **A long stretch of stress or frustration**, since stagnation that sits long enough is said to generate heat. **Overwork without rest**, the fastest route to the deficient version. And **warming tonics taken by someone who does not need them**, which is common in the West. For a person already running hot, they add fuel.

## A day that cools the top and warms the bottom

**Morning.** Warm water first, not cold. A cooked breakfast — congee, oats, soft-cooked egg — plus a cooked cooling vegetable if you are the excess type. If you are depleted, keep the breakfast squarely nourishing.

**Midday.** The largest meal, cooked, with a bitter note if you are an excess type. Ten minutes of walking afterwards, not sitting.

**Afternoon.** Chrysanthemum or mint tea instead of a third coffee. Coffee is warming and moving: fine for a stagnant, cold pattern, unhelpful for someone already running hot.

**Evening.** Eat earlier and lighter, and start winding down an hour before bed rather than in bed. If your feet are cold and your face is hot, a warm foot soak is worth more than any cooling tea — it brings the heat down instead of pushing against it. Warm drinks all day, and finish any hard workout with a warm shower. Most people notice the difference in their nights first.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| My face or chest flushes in the evening, especially when I am tired | ☐ |
| I am thirsty a lot, but cold drinks do not seem to fix it | ☐ |
| I wake between two and four in the morning, or sleep shallowly | ☐ |
| My palms, soles, or chest feel hot at night | ☐ |
| My eyes feel gritty or dry by mid-afternoon | ☐ |
| Cooling food and cold drinks leave my stomach uneasy | ☐ |

Rows one through five with strength and thirst point to excess heat. Those rows plus the last one, plus real fatigue, point to the deficient version — and that is the pattern where cooling harder makes things worse. Either way, the fastest lever is not a food, it is the bedtime.

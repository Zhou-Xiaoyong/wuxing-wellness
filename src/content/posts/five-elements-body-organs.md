The fastest way to misunderstand Chinese medicine is to read its organ names as anatomy. A Western reader sees "the spleen governs digestion" and immediately hits a wall, because the spleen they learned about in biology class is a fist-sized lymphatic organ tucked under the ribs that recycles red blood cells and has essentially nothing to do with breaking down lunch.

Both statements are correct. They're just not talking about the same thing.

Chinese medicine developed largely without dissection as its organizing method. What it built instead was a functional map: five systems, named after organs, each responsible for a bundle of processes that were observed to rise and fall together. The name is a label for the system, not a claim about the tissue. Once that clicks, the whole framework becomes readable — and considerably more interesting.

Here's what each of the five systems is actually said to run inside your body, and how it shows up where you can see it.

One note before we start: this is a traditional model for observing your own patterns, not a diagnostic system. Real symptoms deserve a real clinician, and nothing below is medical advice.

## Wood: the Liver system

**Governs:** the smooth flow of qi, the storage of blood, tendons and ligaments, the eyes.

Think of the Wood system as your body's traffic control. Its defining job isn't filtering toxins — it's keeping everything moving evenly: circulation, digestion's timing, mood, the menstrual cycle. The classical phrase is that the liver "ensures the free coursing of qi," and almost everything attributed to Wood follows from flow being either smooth or stuck.

**On the surface:** tight shoulders and ribs, jaw clenching, tension headaches at the temples, dry or gritty eyes, tendons that feel stiff rather than sore. Emotionally, a shorter fuse than usual.

**When it goes wrong, it's usually stagnation.** Not deficiency — congestion. Which is why the traditional prescriptions for Wood are movement, stretching, sour flavors, and dealing with the thing you're annoyed about rather than storing it.

## Fire: the Heart system

**Governs:** blood circulation, blood vessels, the spirit and mental clarity, the tongue, sleep.

Fire is the most expansive system on the chart. It covers the pump, obviously, but also what the tradition calls shen (神, shén) — consciousness, presence, the quality of being *home* in yourself. That's why sleep and mental clarity are heart matters here rather than brain matters. When the shen is settled you sleep easily and think clearly; when it isn't, you get racing thoughts at midnight and a mind that won't land.

**On the surface:** complexion, the tip of the tongue, sleep onset, palpitations under stress, warmth or coldness in your hands.

Fire is also the seat of connection. Loneliness reads as a Fire matter in this framework, not a psychological footnote — which is one of the more humane ideas in the system.

## Earth: the Spleen and Stomach system

**Governs:** digestion and nutrient extraction, muscle bulk and tone, the mouth and taste, the transformation of food into usable energy.

This is the one that causes the most confusion and the one you'll use most. Earth is the manufacturing floor: it takes food in and turns it into qi and blood. Everything you build after birth comes from here, which is why the tradition calls it the source of postnatal qi and treats it as the pivot the other four depend on.

**On the surface:** appetite, bloating after meals, heavy or tired limbs, loose stools, a coated tongue, muscle tone, the ability to concentrate without fog.

Earth famously dislikes three things: cold food, irregular meals, and worry. Modern nutrition wouldn't put it that way, but the practical advice — eat warm cooked food on a consistent schedule, walk after eating, don't eat while stressed — lines up well enough with what a decent gastroenterologist would say about motility and mealtime habits.

## Metal: the Lung system

**Governs:** breathing and qi, the skin, body hair, the nose, the defensive layer, elimination through the large intestine.

Metal is the boundary system — what comes in, what goes out, and what stays outside. The lungs take in air and distribute qi to the surface, including the protective layer the tradition calls wei qi (卫气, wèi qì), which is the closest classical analogue to immune defense. The skin belongs to this system for the same reason: it's the outermost boundary.

**On the surface:** breath depth, skin dryness or texture, how often you catch what's going around, nasal congestion, bowel regularity.

**Its enemy is dryness.** Autumn dryness (秋燥) is the classic seasonal stress here, and the standard response is moistening food — pear, white fungus, honey — plus deliberate breathing. It's the one element where the seasonal advice is almost embarrassingly literal.

## Water: the Kidney system

**Governs:** essence and constitutional vitality, bones and teeth, hearing and the ears, reproduction, the lower back, fluid metabolism, willpower.

Water is the deepest system and the one that ages. It stores jing (精, jīng) — essence, the constitutional battery you're issued at birth and spend across a lifetime. That single concept absorbs a remarkable range: development in childhood, fertility in adulthood, bone density and hearing in later life, and the baseline stamina that determines how much you can push before you break.

**On the surface:** lower back and knees, energy at the end of a long stretch, hearing, hair, how quickly you recover from a hard week, how much fear you carry.

**Water is slow to rebuild.** This is the crucial practical point. Wood responds to a good workout in a day; Water responds to a season of sleeping properly. The tradition's advice for Water is almost always subtraction — rest, warmth, less stimulation, earlier nights — and that's genuinely unfashionable advice in a culture that prefers supplements.

## How the five systems work as one body

The reason this is a wheel and not a list is that each system feeds and checks the others.

The generating cycle says each system supports the next: Water nourishes Wood, Wood feeds Fire, Fire creates Earth, Earth yields Metal, Metal carries Water. The clinical payoff is the "treat the mother" principle — if lungs are weak, strengthen digestion, because Earth generates Metal. It's a very early version of treating upstream instead of chasing symptoms.

The controlling cycle keeps any one system from dominating. When it fails, the tradition calls it overacting, and the textbook case is Wood overacting on Earth — stress wrecking digestion. Two thousand years before anyone measured cortisol's effect on gut motility, the model had a name for that exact pattern.

The Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon (《黄帝内经》) is where these organ correspondences were first systematically laid out, and later works such as the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) extended them to hundreds of foods. Worth knowing: the assignments were never fully uniform across schools. Treat any single chart as the mainstream reading rather than settled fact.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| One of the five systems describes my recurring complaints better than the others | ☐ |
| I have physical signs that always show up as a group, not alone | ☐ |
| My symptoms shift with the season more than with anything else | ☐ |
| I've been confused before by TCM organ names versus Western anatomy | ☐ |
| I recover more slowly from a hard week than I used to | ☐ |
| I want a way to notice patterns, not a diagnosis | ☐ |

Three or more and this map will give you useful language for something you've already been tracking informally. Fewer, and it's a fascinating window into how a medical tradition thinks — which is worth the reading time on its own.

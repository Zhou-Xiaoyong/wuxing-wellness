Every wellness tradition has a spring story, and most of them converge on the same instinct: eat lighter food, eat more green things, go outside. Chinese medicine is unusually specific about *why*, and that specificity is what makes it useful in a grocery store rather than just inspiring.

Spring belongs to Wood, and Wood's organ is the liver. This is a food and cultural guide, not medical advice. If you have a liver condition or a diagnosed digestive disorder, follow your clinician's guidance and use the ideas below as flavor, not as a protocol.

## Spring is the season of going up

Wood is the phase of rising, spreading, outward movement. Spring is the season when everything that was held tight through winter starts pushing through soil. In the classical texts, that correspondence isn't decorative — the liver is described as the organ that keeps flow moving, and spring is when that flow is naturally at its most insistent.

The practical reading: spring care is about *not blocking* the rise. Food is one of the main ways people either support it or fight it. Heavy, greasy, very rich winter food in March is fighting it. Young greens, sprouts, and fresh shoots support it.

There's a seasonal flavor rule too. The Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon (《黄帝内经》) frames the liver as wanting to spread, nourished by Pungent (辛) and drained by excess Sour (酸). So the traditional spring adjustment isn't "eat more sour" — it's the opposite: go a little lighter on heavily sour and pickled things, and lean on gentle pungency (scallion, chives, a little ginger, fresh herbs) to help things move. A touch of mild Sweet (甘) is traditionally added to soften the edges, since Wood's tendency is toward tension and urgency.

## What changes on the plate

The shift is easier to feel than to memorize. Winter food in this system is warming, dense, salty, slow-cooked. Spring food is:

- **Younger.** Sprouts, shoots, micro-greens, the first thin asparagus — foods that are literally in their growth stage.
- **Greener.** Leafy vegetables move up the priority list.
- **Lighter in cooking method.** More quick stir-fry, blanching, and steaming; less braising and roasting.
- **Slightly sharp or aromatic.** Scallion, garlic chives, cilantro, mint, young ginger, a splash of vinegar at the end rather than a long pickle.
- **A little less meat, and lighter meat.** Not vegetarian by rule — just less of it, and more fish and chicken than lamb and beef.

## The spring shopping list

- **Garlic chives (韭菜, jiǔ cài)** — the archetypal spring vegetable. Pungent, warming, and traditionally associated with supporting the season's upward energy. Best in the first harvests, when they're still tender.
- **Sprouts of all kinds** — mung bean, soybean, and lentil sprouts. Quick to cook, and the growing-shoot quality is the whole point.
- **Shepherd's purse (荠菜, jì cài)** — a wild spring green used across eastern China in dumplings and soups. If you can find it at an Asian market in March, buy it.
- **Bamboo shoots (竹笋, zhú sǔn)** — spring's signature crunch. Slice thin and blanch first.
- **Chrysanthemum greens (茼蒿, tóng hāo)** and other tender Asian greens — mild, slightly aromatic, and perfect for a 90-second stir-fry or a hot pot.
- **Spinach, pea shoots, watercress, and young dandelion greens** — the Western equivalents, and entirely acceptable substitutes.
- **Goji berries (枸杞, gǒu qǐ)** — the classic liver-and-eyes food. A small handful in tea or congee.
- **Rose buds (玫瑰, méi guī) and chrysanthemum (菊花, jú huā)** — the two traditional spring teas, used for the tense, stuck feeling the season can bring.
- **Mild Sweet supports** — Chinese yam (山药, shān yào), jujube dates (红枣, hóng zǎo), millet, and rice. These keep the season's sharper foods from being too much.

## What to ease off in spring

- **Heavy fried and greasy food.** The most consistent piece of advice across the tradition for this season.
- **Heavy sour and heavily pickled foods** in large amounts — the classical caution is about excess, not about a squeeze of lemon.
- **A lot of alcohol.** Spring is when the texts advise moderation most firmly.
- **Very long, very rich braises.** Save them for January.
- **Aggressive "detox" regimens.** The tradition's spring advice is gentle and food-based. A three-day juice fast isn't in it.

## Three days of spring eating

This is a template, not a prescription — swap freely for what's in season near you.

**Day one.** Breakfast: millet or rice congee with a few goji berries and chopped dates. Lunch: a big bowl of soba or rice noodles with blanched pea shoots, sliced bamboo shoots, and a scallion-ginger dressing. Dinner: stir-fried garlic chives with mung bean sprouts, steamed fish, rice.

**Day two.** Breakfast: warm oatmeal with sliced pear. Lunch: brown rice bowl with blanched spinach, a soft-boiled egg, sesame, and a splash of rice vinegar. Dinner: dumplings or wontons with a shepherd's-purse-and-chive filling if you can find the green, or spinach-and-chive if you can't.

**Day three.** Breakfast: congee with Chinese yam. Lunch: leftover grains tossed with watercress, cucumber ribbons, and toasted sesame. Dinner: a light hot pot or a quick soup — chrysanthemum greens, tofu, mushrooms, and a few goji dropped in at the end.

Tea across all three days: chrysanthemum or rose, one cup in the afternoon.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I feel more irritable or impatient in spring than in other seasons | ☐ |
| I get tension headaches, especially at the temples or sides of the head | ☐ |
| My shoulders and jaw tighten when plans change unexpectedly | ☐ |
| Rich, heavy meals sit worse in March than they did in January | ☐ |
| Being outside and moving noticeably resets my mood | ☐ |

Four or five checks means the Wood-spring pattern is a real one for you, and the food shift is worth trying for a few weeks. One or two is ordinary — most people borrow a little Wood in spring regardless of their underlying pattern.

## A realistic boundary

None of this treats anything. What it does is give you a coherent way to decide what to cook in March, built on a couple of millennia of observation. If you have persistent digestive symptoms, jaundice, unexplained fatigue, or anything that worries you, that's a clinician's job. Food is the pleasant part of the conversation, not the whole one.

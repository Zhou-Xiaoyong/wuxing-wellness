It's 6:40 on a Tuesday. The crisper drawer holds a napa cabbage, two daikon and a bag of dried shiitake, and every five elements food chart I find online asks the same thing first: pick an element, then read down to the foods.

Nobody shops that way. You don't walk into a market wanting a Metal vegetable; you walk in with three things already in the bag. So the chart below runs the opposite direction. Find the food first; element, nature and flavor sit in the row beside it, along with one column most food charts leave out.

This is a traditional framework for choosing what to cook, not medical advice. Nothing in these tables diagnoses anything.

## Why the lookup runs the other way

A five elements chart is a diagram of phases, and diagrams read left to right. Food arrives as a vegetable in a plastic bag with a decision attached.

The classical materia medica had the same instinct. The Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) runs to fifty-two scrolls and roughly 1,900 substances, sorted by what things are: waters, fires, minerals, herbs, grains, vegetables, fruit, animals. Not by phase. A working reference is an index of things; the five phases are a layer of interpretation laid over it.

So the table answers three questions at once. Which system is this food filed under. Does it warm me or cool me. What does it taste like.

## The chart

Color first, function second. Green goes to Wood, red to Fire, yellow and orange to Earth, white and pungent to Metal, black and dark to Water.

Then the exceptions. Yam (山药, shān yào) is white and lands under Earth anyway, filed by what it does for digestion rather than by its shade. Cinnamon (肉桂, ròu guì) sits under Water despite being the hottest thing in the spice drawer, because it is said to reach the kidneys.

## The column the other charts drop

Four natures (四气): cold (寒), cool (凉), neutral (平), warm (温), hot (热). Element tells you where a food is filed. Nature tells you what it does to a body on a 38°F morning. Those are different questions, and most charts answer only the first.

Leafy greens are cool, almost all of them. A January salad is a cool dish in a cold month, and that is why it can leave you hunched.

So match the technique to the nature rather than to the ingredient. Cool food wants heat: steam, braise, soup, ginger and scallion into the oil first. Warm food wants a lighter hand, a quick blanch or a vinegar pickle. Braise those January greens with three slices of ginger and they land somewhere else entirely.

## Three foods that are really two foods

**Ginger.** Fresh (生姜, shēng jiāng) is warm and moves outward. Dried (干姜, gān jiāng) is hot and stays in the middle. The Compendium is blunt: raw disperses, cooked harmonizes (生用发散，熟用和中). A chart listing "ginger" once is wrong twice.

**Daikon.** Raw, it's pungent and cool and pushes downward, which is why a few slices close a heavy meal. Cooked, it turns sweet and mild. The isothiocyanates behind that radish burn are heat-labile, so a braise mutes what a grater amplifies.

**Garlic.** Crush it, then wait ten minutes before it meets the pan. Crushing ruptures cells and lets the enzyme alliinase turn alliin into allicin; heat destroys that enzyme, so garlic dropped straight into hot oil makes far less of it. Same clove, different food.

## Working it at the store

November, and the crisper drawer is honest about the season. Napa cabbage, daikon, shiitake: all three Metal, all cool or warm, all sweet. That's a coherent pot waiting to happen.

Shiitake soaking water into the pot. Ginger and scallion into the oil first. Cabbage and daikon in, twenty minutes at a bare simmer. Salt off the heat.

One rule keeps a chart from hardening into a regimen: three foods from a single column in a week is plenty. An index earns its keep through variety, not coverage.

## What a food chart cannot tell you

Dosage. The old texts give weights in units that don't survive a grocery run; the practical versions say a handful, three slices.

Drug interactions, which is the larger gap. Move from greens twice a week to greens daily and your vitamin K intake moves with it, and for anyone on warfarin consistency matters more than quantity. Same caution with kelp and a thyroid medication. A chart cannot see your prescriptions.

And diagnosis, which is not the job. A row in a table is a question about dinner.

## Start with what's already in the drawer

Before printing anything, write down the six foods you ate most this week and look them up. If four of the six land in one row, that is the most useful thing the chart will tell you, and it isn't really about the food.

Then change one. Cook the daikon instead of grating it, or warm the cold lunch through. Small edits, repeated, are how a chart becomes a habit.

If something in your body has been bothering you for weeks, the chart is not the next step. A doctor is. This page has a narrower job: making Tuesday's dinner a better guess than last week's.

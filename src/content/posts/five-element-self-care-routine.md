Almost all self-care advice is written for one kind of person. The cold-plunge-and-five-mile-commute crowd writes for the Wood type. The journal-and-call-a-friend crowd writes for Fire. The meal-prep-and-schedule crowd writes for Earth. None of them is wrong — they are answering a question someone else asked.

Chinese medicine has an unusually tidy answer. Instead of one routine, it has five — Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water — each organized around a different job. The tradition calls them the five elements (五行, wǔ xíng) and uses them less as personality labels than as *phases*: you contain all five, and at any stretch of your life one of them needs feeding. So you never have to decide which routine is correct — you find the phase you are in and work that one.

This is a traditional framework and a lifestyle perspective, not medical advice, and it does not diagnose or treat anything. Exhaustion that does not lift, low mood, or a body that has changed without explanation belongs with a clinician.

## First, find your phase

Ask which sentence sounds most like the last three months: *I feel braced and I cannot get moving* (Wood). *I am running hot and I cannot rest* (Fire). *I am scattered and my digestion is off* (Earth). *I cannot exhale completely and I keep saying yes* (Metal). *I have nothing left* (Water). For the structured version, [How to Know Your Five Element](/blog/how-to-know-your-five-element/) walks through self-observation and the [five-element personality test](/quizzes/five-elements-personality-test/) gives you the quick read. One caution: you rarely need to fix your dominant element — you feed whichever is depleted.

## Wood: movement, direction, and the unhurried exhale

Wood governs the free movement of qi (气, qì) — of mood, of tension, of everything downstream of a liver that is stuck. Its signature complaint is being *braced*: tight jaw, tight shoulders, a habit of sighing, irritability arriving faster than it used to, headaches at the temples.

The care is daily movement rather than intense movement, and the point is not fitness but *flow* — twenty to thirty minutes where your body moves and your attention is not divided. Add a stretch to the ribs and flanks, where a braced body holds. On the food side: Sour (酸) in small, steady amounts — lemon in water, vinegar in a dressing, a sour plum — plus aromatics that move, like rose bud and mint; our [Rose Tea for the Liver](/recipes/rose-tea-liver/) is the classical cup. The habit is planning one thing a week you actually want to do and defending it. The rest is the exhale-heavy breath before meals: four counts in, six out, three minutes.

Running thin looks like snapping at small things, waking at three with a racing mind, or a cycle that has gone strange. More Wood is not the answer — [How to Soothe Liver Qi Naturally](/blog/how-to-soothe-liver-qi-naturally/) is the fuller routine.

## Fire: connection, warmth, and protecting your nights

Fire houses the Heart (心, xīn) and *shen* — the quality of your awareness, whether it settles easily or will not sit down. The Fire question is not "are you happy" but "is warmth moving between you and other people."

The movement is rhythmic and social: dancing badly, a class, a team sport, a brisk walk with someone who talks back — solo intensity is a Wood move, and Fire responds to contact. The food is gently nourishing with a little bitterness — lily bulb (百合, bǎi hé), lotus seed (莲子, lián zǐ), jujube (红枣, hóng zǎo), longan (龙眼, lóng yǎn); [Calming Foods in Chinese Medicine](/blog/calming-foods-chinese-medicine/) is the full pantry. The habit is one real conversation a day with no purpose attached. And the rest is the hour before sleep, protected, because the Heart houses the spirit; [Foods for Better Sleep](/blog/foods-for-better-sleep-tcm/) is the evening version.

Running thin looks like heat with depletion: a flushed, tired face, hot palms and soles, bad sleep. Cool that *gently* — [How to Reduce Internal Heat](/blog/how-to-reduce-internal-heat-chinese-medicine/) explains why cooling alone backfires.

## Earth: rhythm and nourishment

Earth governs the spleen-stomach (脾胃, pí wèi) — turning food into energy, and by extension turning a day into something digestible. Its signature is regularity; its complaint is everything that follows from losing it.

The movement is gentle and frequent: ten minutes of walking after meals beats an hour in the gym on Saturday. The food is warm and cooked, sweet in the wholesome sense — congee, millet, oats, squash, yam (山药, shān yào), cooked root vegetables. The Sweet flavor belongs to Earth, and a squash and a candy bar are not the same input: [Sweet Taste and the Spleen](/blog/sweet-taste-spleen-tcm/) explains the difference. The habit is same times, roughly — meals at consistent hours, a real lunch eaten seated, and eating without a screen, since the middle is trying to process whatever you give it. The rest is eating dinner earlier than you think you should; the late, large dinner is the most reliable cause of morning fog in the tradition's list.

Running thin looks like fatigue that is worse after eating, an appetite that has gone strange, and a mind that loops. If there is also heaviness and a coated taste in the mouth, dampness has joined in — [Foods for a Damp Body](/blog/foods-for-damp-body-chinese-medicine/) is the companion piece.

## Metal: breath and boundaries

Metal governs the lungs and the skin, and its role is the one nobody teaches: taking in what is worth taking in, and letting go of the rest. Its complaint is a life with too much inbound and too little release.

The movement is breathing under load in a way that makes the exhale long: walking uphill, swimming, a slow bike ride. Metal does not want more reps; it wants a fuller breath at the end of them. The food is pungent and gently moistening: ginger, scallion, radish, pear, white fungus. Pungent (辛) disperses, and [Pungent Foods and the Lungs](/blog/pungent-foods-lungs-chinese-medicine/) covers why a little goes a long way. The habit is saying no to one thing this week, out loud, with no paragraph of explanation — Metal governs boundaries the way the skin does. The rest is a long exhale at the end of the day: five or six rounds of double-inhale, long-sigh-out.

Running thin looks like catching everything that goes around, dry skin, a shallow breath, and a quietly held grief. The tradition links Metal to grief more than to any other feeling — [Grief and the Lungs](/blog/grief-and-lungs-chinese-medicine/) treats that directly.

## Water: rest and reserve

Water is the deep reserve — the kidneys' store, what you draw on when nothing else is available. Its complaint is not a symptom so much as an accounting problem: you have spent more than you had.

The movement is restorative rather than stimulating: slow walking, stretching in a warm room, a long soak. Water is the one element where more effort is always the wrong answer — exactly the instinct an ambitious person has to resist. The food is deep-colored and slow-cooked: black beans, black sesame, walnut, seaweed, bone broth, long-simmered soups; [Black Foods and the Kidneys](/blog/black-foods-kidneys-chinese-medicine/) is the pantry and our [Black Bean Kidney Soup](/recipes/black-bean-kidney-soup/) is it in a bowl. The habit is sleep before eleven, most nights — Water is restored almost entirely during sleep, which makes bedtime the highest-leverage habit in the system. The rest is doing less on purpose, once a week: not a productive rest, an empty one.

Running thin looks like a cold lower back, waking at three, and a tank that refills more slowly than it used to.

## One week, five ways

Run one section's four items for a week — the phase you are actually in, not all five at once, since five elements applied simultaneously is just another to-do list. The one move that helps all five is going to bed an hour earlier: it is Wood's rest, Fire's protected hour, Earth's rhythm, Metal's long exhale, and Water's entire job.

## Quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| I feel braced and tight, and I cannot get moving | ☐ |
| I am running hot and I cannot rest | ☐ |
| I am scattered, and my digestion has gone irregular | ☐ |
| I cannot exhale completely, and I keep saying yes | ☐ |
| I have nothing left in the tank | ☐ |
| I have tried to fix this by doing more, and it did not work | ☐ |

Whichever row you checked first is the phase to work for the next seven days. If it was the last row, start with sleep and subtract something before adding anything.

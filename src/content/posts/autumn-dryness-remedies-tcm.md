There's a particular kind of rough you feel in October that isn't a cold. Dry throat, lips that split no matter how much balm you use, skin that drinks lotion and asks for more, a dry tickly cough that shows up around 4 p.m. and vanishes by morning. Western medicine doesn't have a tidy name for it. Chinese medicine has been calling it autumn dryness (秋燥) for centuries, and it has a specific toolkit for exactly this.

This is a food and home-remedy guide, not medical advice. A cough that lasts more than three weeks, any wheezing, breathlessness, coughing up blood, or anything layered on top of asthma or COPD belongs with a clinician. What follows is the low-stakes, pleasant end of the season — what to reach for when the air turns sharp.

## Why autumn is the dry season

In five-element theory, autumn is the Metal season, and Metal's organ is the lungs. The lungs are described as the most delicate organ, the one that "descends and moistens" — they're supposed to deliver fluid down through the body. When autumn dries the air outside, that descending moisture has less to work with, and you feel it first in the obvious places: throat, nose, lips, skin. The classical goal of the season's remedies is one word — *moistening* — and it means food and habits with a soft, silky, slightly gelatinous character, not just drinking more water.

## The remedy list

**Stewed pear (梨, lí).** The single most-recommended autumn food in China, and the first thing to try. Core a pear, add a little rock sugar and maybe a few goji berries (枸杞, gǒu qǐ), steam it until soft, and eat it warm with the juice. The tradition prefers it cooked once the weather turns properly cold. It's the household answer to a dry throat.

**Snow fungus soup (银耳, yín ěr).** The texture star and the archetype of "moistening." Soaked and simmered until silky and slightly gelatinous, it turns a plain pot into the exact quality the season calls for. Cheap, shelf-stable, available at any Asian market. Our [pear and snow-fungus soup](/recipes/pear-snow-fungus-soup/) is the canonical version.

**Honey in warm water (蜂蜜, fēng mì).** The simplest moistening food in the pantry, and the one with real modern evidence behind it — several reviews have found honey performs as well as or better than common over-the-counter options for acute cough in adults and older children. Stir it into warm, not boiling, water. Never give honey to an infant under one year.

**Lily bulb and lotus root (百合 / 莲藕, bǎi hé / lián ǒu).** The quiet, starchy moisteners. Lily bulb is traditionally aimed at a dry cough and a restless mind at once; lotus root, slow-simmered in broth, turns sweet and translucent. Both simmer into any mild soup.

**A bedroom humidifier.** Not a food, but the single most effective thing on this list. The tradition's instinct — add moisture to the air, don't just drink water — is exactly what a humidifier delivers, and it does more for October dryness than any soup.

**Chrysanthemum tea (菊花, jú huā).** For the early-autumn version of the season, when summer heat hasn't fully left and the dryness comes with a hot, flushed head. Cooling, floral, easy. ## Two home practices worth trying

**Steam inhalation.** A bowl of just-boiled water, a towel tent over your head, slow breaths for a minute or two — the old-fashioned way to put moisture directly where the lungs meet the air. Keep your eyes closed and don't scald yourself. Do it once in the evening when the throat is the part that complains.

**Warm liquids all day.** Not iced. The season's whole logic runs opposite to summer's cold-and-raw habit; warm liquids keep the descending-moistening function from having to fight the temperature of what you drink. A spoon of honey in the evening cup is the targeted version.

## What to ease off

The subtractions matter as much as the additions:

- **Cold and raw food.** Salads and iced drinks were correct in July. In October the tradition wants warm, cooked, and moist.
- **Very spicy and heavily drying food.** A little pungency supports Metal; a plate of chili does the opposite of moistening.
- **Deep-fried and heavily roasted food.** These carry dryness in the classical reading, and autumn already has enough.
- **Too much bitter and too much alcohol.** Both were summer's tools and now work against you; alcohol is drying in the traditional framing and dehydrating in the modern one.

## A quick self-check

| Statement | Feels like me |
| --- | --- |
| My lips, nose, or throat go dry as soon as the weather turns | ☐ |
| I get a dry, tickly cough in autumn without being properly sick | ☐ |
| My skin gets tight, flaky, or itchy in October and November | ☐ |
| I wake with a dry mouth or a scratchy throat | ☐ |
| Warm fluids and a humidifier noticeably help my throat | ☐ |
| This hits me emotionally too — nostalgia or low mood | ☐ |

Four or more checks and the Metal-autumn dryness pattern is genuinely yours, and the moistening list is worth running for a few weeks. If mainly the emotional row landed, the tradition's advice is the same either way — warm food, slow breathing, and one small thing released on purpose.

## The honest limit

Food and steam don't treat a respiratory condition. What they do, reliably, is make a dry season more comfortable, and they happen to converge with plain good practice: humidified air, honey for a cough, warm fluids, less alcohol. The framework's real value is that it tells you *when* to make the switch — the tradition changes the whole kitchen in early autumn rather than waiting for symptoms to arrive.

Anything that lasts, worsens, or scares you is a clinician's job. Everything else is dinner — ideally with a steamed pear in it.

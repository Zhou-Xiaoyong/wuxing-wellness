The first warm week of March does something to people. Coats come off at 50°F, windows open, and by Thursday you are short with the dog.

In five-element thinking that is the season arriving in the body: liver qi rising with the light, nowhere to go. The old books call the result constraint (郁, yù). Stuck, not sad.

Something sits at the base of your throat that will not go down or come up. Chinese medicine calls it plum-pit qi (梅核气, méi hé qì), and this cup is built on the blossom of the plum.

It is a ritual, not a treatment. Ten minutes, a warm cup, a reason to sit still at the hour you usually push through.

## Spring wants movement, not a scrub

Liver teas sold in the West are mostly cooling or bitter, and both have a place. The [simmered three-herb cup](/recipes/chinese-herbal-tea-for-liver/) is right when there is heat: red eyes, a bitter taste on waking, an ache behind the eyes.

This is the other picture, and in March it is the common one. No redness, no bitterness in the mouth. A band of tightness around the ribs, a habit of sighing, a fuse that shortened over the winter. What that wants is free flow (疏泄, shū xiè), and the tool is aromatic, not bitter. Bitters push down; nothing here is stuck downward.

## What goes in the cup

| Role | Herb | Amount | What it is doing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The lead | Green-calyx plum blossom (绿萼梅, lǜ è méi) | 1 tbsp, about 2 g | Floral, faintly almond, the classic for a throat that will not clear |
| The mover | Finger citron (佛手, fó shǒu) | 2 thin slices | Citrus oil that unsticks things sideways rather than down |
| The base | Tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí) | 1 strip | Keeps the cup from landing cold on an empty stomach |
| The top note | Mint (薄荷, bó hé) | 4 leaves | Lifts it toward the head, and is volatile, so it goes in last |

The blossom is picked in late winter, before the leaf is out. The Qing supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目拾遗》, 1765) gives it one job, soothing the liver and resolving constraint (疏肝解郁).

A tablespoon of dried buds weighs two grams, so brewing it like black tea leaves gets you hot water.

## Ingredients

- 1 tbsp dried green-calyx plum blossoms (绿萼梅, lǜ è méi), about 2 g
- 2 thin dried finger citron slices (佛手, fó shǒu), each about an inch across
- 1 strip dried tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí), about an inch long
- 8 oz water at 195°F
- 4 fresh mint leaves (薄荷, bó hé), or a scant 1/4 tsp dried
- 1 small piece rock sugar (冰糖, bīng táng), optional

## Method

1. Warm the cup with hot tap water, then pour it out. A cold cup pulls the water under 190°F.
2. Peel and finger citron in first. They are the dense pieces.
3. Water to 195°F, small bubbles and no rolling boil, over the peel. Cover, 3 minutes.
4. Add blossoms and mint. Cover, 90 seconds. Longer and the mint goes flat.
5. Strain into a second cup and smell it before the first sip.
6. Steep the peel and citron again at 195°F for 2 minutes with a fresh pinch of blossom.

## Two temperatures, ninety seconds

Most failures here are one decision: boiling water.

Menthol melts at 108°F and is volatile enough that a rolling boil drives it off with the steam inside two minutes, which is why mint tea poured straight from the kettle tastes of grass. At 195°F, ninety seconds, you keep the cool feeling at the back of your mouth.

Rind wants the opposite: the oils sit in cells heat has to rupture, and three minutes is enough for an inch-long strip.

Cover the cup both times; the aromatics are the ones that escape.

## When to reach for it

Mid-afternoon on a bright, windy day when your jaw is tight and you have sighed four times in an hour; the week a project stalls and your shoulders climb; any March afternoon when the alternative is a third coffee.

## The detox question, answered plainly

"Liver detox tea" is marketing. Your liver runs its own two-stage chemistry on everything you eat, and no flower speeds it up. The food with real enzyme-induction data is a brassica: broccoli sprouts, whose sulforaphane raises phase II enzyme activity in human trials. Not a blossom.

Peppermint oil has trial support for bloating in irritable bowel, though those studies used enteric-coated capsules, not a mug. Finger citron peel oil is mostly limonene, the compound behind the smell of orange peel. Neither is a cleanse.

Skip the mint if you have reflux; it relaxes the valve at the top of the stomach. One or two cups a day is the ceiling, and leave it out in pregnancy unless your provider says otherwise. Yellowing eyes, dark urine, pain under the right ribs, or fatigue building for weeks belong to a doctor, not a teapot.

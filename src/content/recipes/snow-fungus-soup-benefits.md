There is a bowl that shows up in Chinese households the way chicken soup shows up in American ones: white, faintly sweet, slippery, and given to anyone who looks tired, dry, or recently recovered from something. It is snow fungus soup, and it costs almost nothing to make.

Snow fungus (银耳, yín ěr) is a dried mushroom that has no flavor worth speaking of. What it has instead is texture — soaked and simmered, it turns into a soft, gelatinous frill that gives the broth body without meat, cream, or thickener. In the five-element system it belongs to Metal, the phase of the lungs, the skin, and the season of dryness. If you have ever spent a week in heated indoor air and found your throat scratchy, your lips cracked, and your skin papery, you have met the pattern this bowl is aimed at.

This is food, not medicine.

## What it is traditionally used for

The Chinese name for snow fungus in old recipe books is the "poor man's bird's nest" (平民燕窝, píng mín yàn wō) — a nod to the costly swallow's nest used for the same moistening work. The tradition gives it three jobs.

The first is moisture. Metal governs the lungs and the skin, and dry autumn air is said to injure both first: a dry tickly cough, a throat that feels scraped, a nose that bleeds in winter, skin that flakes at the cheeks. Snow fungus is classified as a yin-nourishing food — sweet, neutral, and slightly cooling — so it is given as a broth rather than a tea, because the point is volume of fluid as much as the ingredient.

The second is the voice. Singers and teachers in China are famously told to drink it. A hoarse, tired voice after days of talking is read as lung dryness, and a warm slippery bowl is easier on a raw throat than water alone.

Third, it is a gentle food for convalescence: after a fever, after a chest infection, during a stretch when everything feels too heavy. It has almost no flavor, which is a virtue when you are recovering.

The modern read is undramatic: mostly soluble fiber and polysaccharides, a few minerals, almost no calories of its own. The gel is real, the hydration is real, and the rest of the benefit is the benefit of any warm, lightly sweet food taken slowly.

## Ingredients

- 15 g dried snow fungus (银耳, yín ěr) — one large head
- 1.2 litres cold water
- 8 dried red dates (红枣, hóng zǎo), pitted
- 40 g yellow rock sugar, or to taste
- 1 tbsp dried goji berries (枸杞, gǒu qǐ)

## Method

1. Put the dried fungus in a bowl of cold water and leave it 3–4 hours, until it has swelled to several times its size and feels like soft jelly.
2. Cut away the hard yellow base with scissors — it never softens and it makes the soup taste of nothing.
3. Tear the frills into small pieces with your fingers rather than cutting them; torn edges release more of the gel.
4. Put the torn fungus in a pot with the cold water and the dates. Bring to a boil, then reduce to a low simmer.
5. Simmer 45 minutes with the lid ajar, until the liquid has thickened slightly and the frills are translucent.
6. Add the rock sugar and stir until it dissolves, then simmer 10 more minutes.
7. Turn off the heat, scatter in the goji berries, and let the pot stand 5 minutes before serving.
8. Serve warm, or chill and serve cold in summer. Keeps four days in the fridge.

## Three things that decide whether it works

**Soak in cold water, never hot.** Hot water softens the outside fast and leaves the center tough, and the gel comes out thin. Cold water for three to four hours gives an even, jelly-like rehydration.

**Tear, don't chop.** The gel comes from surface area. Fingers make irregular edges; a knife makes clean ones. Small pieces also mean you get a thick soup in an hour instead of three.

**Sugar goes in late.** Added at the start, sugar raises the boiling point and keeps the frills firm. Added near the end, the soup thickens properly.

## When to reach for it

- The first cold, dry week of autumn, when your throat starts scratching at night.
- After a long stretch of teaching, podcasting, or talking on calls all day.
- Heated indoor air has left your skin tight and your lips cracked.
- You are getting over a cold and solid food still feels like too much work.

It is not a fit for everyone. If you run damp and heavy — a coated tongue, bloating, loose stools, a feeling of wetness in the chest — a sweet gelatinous bowl can make you feel more sluggish, and the tradition would send you toward barley and adzuki instead. If you are diabetic, note that most of the sweetness here is added sugar.

This is a moistening ritual, not a treatment. A cough that lasts more than three weeks, blood in the sputum, or a voice that stays hoarse without a cold belongs with a doctor.

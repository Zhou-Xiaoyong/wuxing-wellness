The radiator clicks on for the first time in November. By the second evening your throat feels scraped and at six o'clock you want something hot and salty. Not a dessert.

In a Cantonese kitchen that is a soup which goes on the dinner table (雪梨汤, xuě lí tāng): pork ribs, sweet apricot kernels, a strip of aged tangerine peel, two pears near the end. The broth is amber and clear, faintly sweet from the fruit.

The site already has the sweet bowl, [pear and snow-fungus soup](/recipes/pear-snow-fungus-soup/). This is the other half.

It is a ritual, not a treatment. Ninety minutes of a pot ticking on the stove is most of the point in a month when everything else speeds up.

## The pear changes jobs when you salt it

The Compendium of Materia Medica (《本草纲目》) splits the fruit in two: raw to clear the heat of the six bowels, cooked to nourish the yin of the five organs (生者清六腑之热，熟者滋五脏之阴). Autumn is when the second half of that line becomes the useful one.

Put a pear in sugar and it softens into syrup. Put the same pear in pork broth and it holds its shape, goes translucent at the edges, and gives up a sweetness closer to turnip than to dessert.

Classical dietary writing files this under moistening the lung (润肺, rùn fèi), what the autumn kitchen adds as the air dries (秋燥, qiū zào). You do not have to buy the mechanism to notice what the bowl does.

## What goes in the pot

| Component | Amount | What it is doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pork ribs | 1 lb, cut across the bone | Body and salt; the bone gives the broth its weight |
| Asian pear (雪梨, xuě lí) | 2, peeled, in for the last 25 minutes | Sweetness, and a soft bite that survives cooking |
| Sweet apricot kernels (南杏, nán xìng) | 1/4 cup | The almond note; the standard partner to pear in Cantonese soup |
| Dried tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí) | 1 strip | Cuts the pork smell, keeps the pot from tasting flat |
| Fresh ginger (生姜, shēng jiāng) | 3 slices | Warmth at the back of the throat |

Apricot kernels need their own warning, because the bag you find online may be the wrong one. Sweet kernels (南杏) are mild and marzipan-scented, used by the handful. Bitter kernels (北杏) are the medicinal ones, sold in pharmacies for formulas, and they carry amygdalin, which releases cyanide when chewed. Sweet only, here. The [autumn foods list](/blog/autumn-foods-for-lungs/) makes the same point about the almond family.

## Ingredients

- 1 lb pork ribs, cut across the bone into 2-inch pieces
- 2 Asian pears (雪梨, xuě lí), about 1 lb, or 2 firm Bosc pears
- 1/4 cup sweet apricot kernels (南杏, nán xìng), about 1.2 oz
- 1 strip dried tangerine peel (陈皮, chén pí), about 2 inches
- 3 slices fresh ginger (生姜, shēng jiāng)
- 6 cups cold water
- 3/4 tsp fine sea salt, or to taste

## Method

1. Ribs into cold water, boil hard 3 minutes, dump, rinse the foam off each piece, wash the pot.
2. Soak the peel 10 minutes in warm water and scrape the pith off.
3. Ribs back in the clean pot with cold water, kernels, peel and ginger. Boil, then drop to a bare simmer.
4. Simmer 90 minutes, lid ajar, skimming once at 20.
5. Peel and quarter the pears; add them for the last 25 minutes.
6. Salt off the heat. Serve with a piece of pear and a couple of ribs in each bowl.

## Three decisions that decide the bowl

**Blanch from cold.** Ribs into cold water, bring it up, boil hard three minutes, dump and rinse. That grey foam is coagulated protein; left in, it clouds the broth and leaves a smell people call porky. Cantonese cooks call the step flying water (飞水, fēi shuǐ).

**A bare simmer, never a rolling boil.** Boiling shatters fat into droplets that stay suspended, which is why a hard-boiled broth looks milky. Ninety minutes at a shiver, lid ajar, gives clear amber.

**Pear in at twenty-five minutes.** Long enough to soften, short enough to keep a shape. When it is right, a spoon meets slight resistance and the piece still looks like a piece.

## When to reach for it

The evening after a dry, windy walk, when your throat feels sanded. The week the heat comes on and the whole house starts clearing its throat. A Sunday with ninety minutes in it.

## The honest limit

Pork broth and cooked pear will not clear a cough that has been around for a month. Wheezing, breathlessness, blood, or fever belongs with a clinician, and a dry cough past three weeks is worth a visit.

A medium pear carries around 5.5 grams of fiber, most of it soluble pectin, plus the water it cooked in. That is a duller account of moistening than the classical one, and probably the true one.

If you watch your sodium, halve the salt and squeeze lemon in at the table. Keep the bitter kernels out of the kitchen entirely.
